Chapter 15 · Part 1

The algorithmic coding round

Format the most common screen, and part of most onsitesabout 30 minutes8 quick checks · 5 exercises

Knowing the data structures is half of a coding round. The other half is running the 45 minutes: what to say first, when to start typing, how to test, what to do when you're stuck. People who know enough still fail by skipping steps under pressure. This chapter is the playbook, and the method for the two timed problems you'll do every day.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Say the four things a coding interviewer scores, and how each one shows.
  2. Split 45 minutes into phases, with a time for each.
  3. Use the input size to guess the complexity the interviewer expects.
  4. Go from a brute force to a better solution by asking "what work is repeated?".
  5. Test your own code by hand and find your bug before the interviewer does.
  6. Get unstuck, and run a timed drill that actually improves you.

How to read this: once, early in week 1, then again the day before each coding mock. It uses Big-O from How fast is your code?; the worked examples use hash maps, stacks, heaps and sorting from the week 1 chapters, but you can follow the method without them.

1The problem: solved it, still failed

Sam gets "find the length of the longest run of consecutive numbers in an unsorted list". He recognizes it. He says "OK" and starts typing. Eighteen minutes later he has a working solution: put everything in a set, and start counting only from numbers that have no smaller neighbor. He says, "Done, I think."

The interviewer asks: "What's the complexity? What happens with an empty list? With duplicates?" Sam isn't sure the empty case works. He runs through it, finds a bug, fixes it. "It's O(n), I think, because of the set."

The feedback: "Got to a solution, but didn't clarify, didn't explain the approach before coding, couldn't state complexity confidently, and didn't test. Lean no hire."

Sam solved the problem. He still lost, because a solved problem is only one of four things being measured. The interviewer had almost nothing to write about the other three: they couldn't see how he thought, because he didn't say it, and they had to find his bug for him.

Sam's code was correct in the end. What would most have changed the outcome?

Right. Those are the visible parts of problem solving, communication and testing. The same code, with those around it, is a hire.

Imagine a maths exam where only the final answer is written down. The grader can't tell if you understood it or got lucky, and if there's a mistake they can't give partial credit. A coding interview is graded the same way, except the "working" is what you say out loud. Speed was not his problem: 18 minutes is fine. Comments don't replace explaining the idea before you code, or finding your own bug.

2What gets scored

Most companies score coding rounds on about four signals. The names differ; the content doesn't:

SignalWhat the interviewer writes down
Problem solvingDid you clarify, find a working approach, improve it, and know its cost? How much help did you need?
Code qualityIs the code correct, readable, well named, split into sensible pieces? Would it pass code review?
TestingDid you check it yourself, with good cases (normal, edge, tricky), and find your own bugs?
CommunicationCould the interviewer follow your thinking at every moment? Did you take hints well?

Notice that three of the four are about what happens around the code. That's good news: they're habits, and habits can be drilled. The rest of this chapter is those habits, in the order you use them.

Four signals: problem solving, code quality, testing, communication. Correct code is necessary, not enough.

3The clock: 45 minutes in phases

A pilot on a long flight doesn't wonder whether they're on time; they check against waypoints. Give the round waypoints too. For a 45-minute round with one problem:

MinutesPhaseDone when
0 – 5Understand: restate, ask questions, work one exampleYou and the interviewer agree on the input, output and an example
5 – 12Plan: brute force and its cost, then a better idea and its costThe interviewer agrees with the approach ("sounds good, go ahead")
12 – 30Code, while saying what each part is forThe code is complete
30 – 40Test by hand: trace an example, then edge cases; fix bugsYou've traced it and fixed what you found
40 – 45Complexity, follow-ups, your questionsTime

The waypoint people miss most is minute 12: they start typing at minute 2. Coding without an agreed plan is the most expensive mistake in the round, because if the idea is wrong, you discover it at minute 25 with no time left to change course. The second most missed is minute 30: people code until the end and never test. If you're behind at a waypoint, say so and adapt: "I'm going to code the simpler O(n log n) version first, then improve it if there's time."

Some rounds have two problems in 45 to 60 minutes. Then the first problem is usually easier: aim to finish it in about 20 minutes, with the same phases squeezed.

It's minute 8. You have a brute force that works, and a hunch about a faster approach but haven't worked it out. What do you do?

Right. You've already banked the brute force by stating it. Now you explore, out loud, with a deadline. Interviewers often nudge here if they hear your reasoning.

Think of the waypoints as a budget. Coding the brute force at minute 8 spends most of your time on the version that scores lowest, when the better idea may be two minutes away. Thinking silently burns the same minutes, and the interviewer can't help or give credit for reasoning they can't hear. The middle path keeps both: say the brute force (that's on the record), think out loud toward the better one, and fall back by a set time.

4Understand: questions that change the answer

Problems are stated a little vaguely on purpose. Asking the right questions is part of the score, and the answers often change which solution is right. Ask about:

  • Size: how many items, how big are the values? This one tells you the target complexity (below).
  • Shape: sorted or not? Negative numbers? Duplicates? Empty input possible?
  • Output: return what exactly? Any valid answer or a specific one? Indexes or values? What if there's no answer?
  • Changes: is the input given all at once, or as a stream? Can I modify it?

Then work one small example by hand, out loud, before planning. It catches misunderstandings while they cost nothing.

The size question deserves its own table. A computer does very roughly 10⁷ to 10⁸ simple Python steps per second, and interview problems are usually set so the intended solution runs in about a second. So the size hints at the cost they expect:

n up to aboutLikely targetTypical shape
10 to 20O(2ⁿ) or O(n!)try every subset or order (backtracking)
a few hundredO(n³)three nested loops, some dynamic programming
a few thousandO(n²)every pair
10⁵ to 10⁶O(n log n) or O(n)sort, heap, hash map, one pass
10⁹ or moreO(log n) or O(1)binary search, maths

It's a hint, not a law: interviewers sometimes don't give sizes, and then "what's the largest input?" is a good question in itself.

You're told n can be up to 200,000. Your idea compares every pair. What does that tell you?

Right. n²/2 at n = 200,000 is 2 × 10¹⁰. At 10⁷ to 10⁸ steps a second that's minutes to over half an hour. Say the brute force anyway, then use the size to justify looking for something better.

Do the arithmetic out loud: 200,000 × 200,000 / 2 = 20 billion pairs. Python manages roughly 10 to 100 million simple steps a second, so that's 200 to 2,000 seconds: far too slow. A faster language buys maybe 10× to 50×; the gap between n² and n log n here is about 10,000×. The size is telling you which family of solutions they have in mind.

5Plan: brute force first, then "what work is repeated?"

Always say the brute force first, with its cost, even if you know a better way. It proves you understand the problem, it's a fallback, and it gives you something to improve. Then ask one question of it: what work does the brute force do over and over? Most improvements remove repeated work by remembering something.

Worked example, "Two Sum": given a list and a target, find two positions whose values add up to the target.

  1. Brute force: try every pair. O(n²) time, O(1) extra space.
  2. What's repeated? For each number x, the inner loop scans the whole list looking for target − x. We search for things again and again.
  3. Remove it: remember what we've seen in a hash map from value to position. Then "is target − x there?" is one O(1) lookup.
  4. New cost: O(n) time, O(n) space. We traded memory for time, and we say so.

The same question, applied to the problems you'll meet this week, leads to a handful of tools:

The repeated workWhat removes itChapter
searching for a value again and againa hash map or setHash maps
looking back for the nearest bigger or smaller itema stack (monotonic stack)Linear structures
finding the smallest or largest again after each changea heapHeaps
comparing everything with everythingsort first, so related items sit togetherSorting
finding items in order, or the next one after xa balanced tree or a sorted list with binary searchTrees
re-solving the same smaller problemremember answers (@cache)Python toolkit, and week 3

Before you code, say the plan in two or three sentences and the cost, then get a nod: "I'll keep a dict from value to index, and for each number check whether its complement is there. That's O(n) time and space. Shall I code that?"

Brute force out loud, with its cost. Then find the repeated work, and remember something so you don't repeat it. Say the plan and get agreement before you type.

"For each day's temperature, how many days until a warmer one?" The brute force scans forward from each day. What's the repeated work, and the tool?

Right: the monotonic stack from the linear structures chapter. Each day enters the stack once and leaves once, so O(n) instead of O(n²).

Picture the scans as arrows starting from each day and moving right until they hit a warmer day. For a cooling stretch like 70, 69, 68, 67 followed by 75, every arrow runs over the same days to reach 75. That's the repeated work. If instead you keep the "still waiting" days in a stack, a single 75 answers all of them at once as it pops them. Sorting loses the order of the days, which the answer depends on, and a hash map finds equal values, not the next warmer one.

6Code: narrate the why, not the what

While you type, keep a light running commentary, the kind a driving instructor gives: not "I'm pressing the brake" but "slowing because the light's about to change". Say what each block is for: "this loop builds the counts", "this is the edge case where the list is empty". Don't read the code aloud character by character.

  • Write the function signature first and the return value, then fill in the middle.
  • Use helpers for named steps (in_bounds(r, c)), even if you write them last. "I'll assume a helper that does X and write it at the end" is fine.
  • Good names from the first line: seen, left, best. Renaming later wastes time.
  • Use the language well: the tools in the Python toolkit chapter make code shorter and easier to check.
  • If you go quiet to think, say so: "give me 30 seconds to think about the loop bounds."

7Test by hand: be your own reviewer

When the code is done, don't say "done". Say "let me test it", and do it in two passes.

Pass 1, trace: run a small normal example through the code line by line, writing the variables as they change. Use the example from minute 3. Read what the code says, not what you meant: that's where the bugs are.

nums = [2, 7, 11], target = 9
i=0  x=2   need 7   seen={}        → not found; seen={2: 0}
i=1  x=7   need 2   seen={2: 0}    → found 0 → return [0, 1]  ✓

Pass 2, edge cases: pick the ones that break code most often, and walk each through quickly:

  • empty input, one item;
  • duplicates (Two Sum with [3, 3], target 6);
  • no valid answer;
  • all the same, already sorted, reverse sorted;
  • negative numbers, zero, the largest values.

Finding your own bug is a positive signal: it's exactly what the testing score is for. When you find one, say what it was and why the fix is right, then re-trace the case that broke.

You've finished coding with 12 minutes left. Best next move?

Right. Twelve minutes is plenty for both passes. You turn "I think it works" into "it works on these cases, and here's why".

"I think it works" hands the testing job to the interviewer, and they'll do it, but you get no credit and they'll find the bug. Many interviews don't run code at all, so asking them to run it may not be an option. Test it the way a careful reviewer would: one normal case traced through every line, then the edge cases where code usually breaks. That's the testing signal, and it usually finds a bug in time to fix it.

8When you're stuck

Everyone gets stuck sometimes. What's scored is what you do next. Try these in order, out loud:

  1. Go smaller. Solve it by hand for 3 items, then 4. Watch what you do; that's often the algorithm.
  2. Go back to the brute force and ask again what it repeats (section 5).
  3. Walk the toolbox: would a hash map help? Sorting first? A stack? A heap? Two pointers (week 2)? Saying the list out loud often triggers the idea.
  4. Change the problem: what if the input were sorted? What if there were no duplicates? Solve the easier version, then see what breaks.
  5. Take the hint well. If the interviewer offers one, take it, say how it changes your plan, and keep going. Needing one small hint costs a little; ignoring it, or arguing, costs a lot.

You've been stuck for three minutes. The interviewer says, "What if the array were sorted?" What do you do?

Right. A hint is a door; walk through it out loud. Often the answer is "sort first, O(n log n), then do the easy sorted version".

Hints in interviews are rarely random. "What if it were sorted?" usually means "sorting is part of the answer" or "there's a sorted-case trick that adapts". Pushing back treats the hint as a mistake, and asking for the full answer gives up the problem-solving signal entirely. Following it, out loud, shows that you can take an idea and run with it, which is what working with a colleague looks like.

9The daily drill: practice that changes something

Every week of the track has two timed problems a day. Doing a problem without a timer and peeking when stuck feels productive, but it trains you to solve problems slowly with help, which is the opposite of the interview. Run each drill like a small mock:

  1. 25-minute timer (35 for a hard one). Pick from the drill list under that day's chapter on the track.
  2. Out loud, the whole time, even alone: restate, ask yourself the size and edge questions, brute force and cost, better approach and cost, then code.
  3. Test by hand before you run it (section 7). Then run it.
  4. If the timer ends, stop. Read one hint or the first idea of a solution, close it, and finish on your own.
  5. Log it: one line in sections/15-interview-formats/notes.md: date, problem, minutes, solved alone or with a hint, and the one thing to fix. Redo any problem you needed help with three days later, from scratch.

The log is what makes it improve you: after a week, the "thing to fix" column shows your real weak spot ("I never ask about duplicates", "my loop bounds are off by one"), and that's what to drill next.

You can't solve a drill in 25 minutes. What's the most useful thing to do?

Right. You get the idea you were missing, you still write it yourself, and the redo checks whether it stuck. That's spaced practice.

Picture learning a song on guitar. Struggling for an hour on one bar trains frustration more than the bar; reading the sheet music and never playing it trains nothing. What works: look up just the chord you're missing, play it yourself, and come back to the song in a few days to see if it stuck. A drill works the same way: a small hint, your own code, a logged lesson, and a redo.

10What to say in the interview

  1. "Let me make sure I understand": restate it, ask about size, duplicates, empty input and the output, and work one example.
  2. "The brute force is…, which is O(…). The repeated work is…, so I'll use… instead, which makes it O(…) time and O(…) space. Shall I code that?"
  3. Code with a running "what this part is for".
  4. "Let me test it": trace the example, then the edge cases; fix and say why.
  5. Close with the final complexity, and one follow-up you'd consider (a stream, a huge input, many machines).

✓What to remember

  1. Four signals: problem solving, code quality, testing, communication. Correct code alone is a lean no.
  2. Waypoints: understand by 5, plan agreed by 12, code by 30, tested by 40.
  3. Ask about size, shape, output and changes. n ≈ 10⁵ to 10⁶ means O(n log n) or better.
  4. Brute force first with its cost; then "what work is repeated?" and remember something.
  5. Narrate the why. Signature first, helpers, good names.
  6. Test in two passes: trace a normal case, then empty, one, duplicates, no answer.
  7. Stuck: smaller example, back to brute force, walk the toolbox, easier version, take hints.
  8. Drills: 25-minute timer, out loud, test before running, hint only after time, log, redo in three days.

✎Exercises

Warm-up · pen and paper

1. Read the size

For each limit, name the likely target complexity and one kind of approach: (a) n ≤ 15 (b) n ≤ 2,000 (c) n ≤ 500,000 (d) values up to 10¹², asking for a square root rounded down (e) a 100 × 100 grid.

Answer

(a) O(2ⁿ): try every subset, 32,768 of them. (b) O(n²): every pair, 2 million. (c) O(n log n) or O(n): sort, heap, hash map, one pass. (d) O(log n) in the value: binary search on the answer, about 40 steps. (e) 10,000 cells: O(cells) or O(cells · log cells), a walk over the grid; even O(cells²) = 10⁸ would be slow in Python.
