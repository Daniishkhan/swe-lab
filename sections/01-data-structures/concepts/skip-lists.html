Chapter 1 · Part 11

Express lanes for a linked list

P2 skip lists, and why Redis uses themabout 50 minutes10 quick checks · 8 exercises

A sorted linked list is almost a great structure. Nothing ever shifts, a new key goes in by changing two pointers, and reading keys in order is a plain walk. Its one flaw is that it can't jump: to find anything, you walk from the start. A skip list fixes exactly that flaw by stacking express lanes on top of the list, and it decides which keys get express stops by flipping coins. The result searches, inserts and deletes in O(log n) on average, like a balanced tree, with no rotations at all. That simplicity is why it sits inside Redis sorted sets, Java's ConcurrentSkipListMap, and the in-memory write buffers of LevelDB and RocksDB.

  5. Tune the coin's probability p, and say why Redis and LevelDB pick 1/4.
  6. Answer range and rank queries with spans, and explain why Redis, Java and RocksDB chose skip lists over balanced trees.

1The problem: a sorted list you can't jump into

You keep a game's leaderboard as a sorted linked list of scores. You picked it for good reasons. A new score goes into the middle by changing two pointers; nothing shifts, unlike an array, where inserting near the front moves everything after it. And "show the top 100" is a walk from the head.

At 1,000 players it was instant. Now there are 1,000,000 players and 20,000 new scores a second. The pointer change is still two writes. Finding where to make it is the problem: a linked list can't jump to the middle, so every new score walks from the head past about 500,000 nodes on average. 20,000 × 500,000 is 10 billion hops a second. Each hop follows a pointer to some other place in memory, which is often a trip to RAM of about 100 ns. That's about 1,000 seconds of work every second.

You know one fix from Sorted and fast: a balanced binary search tree finds the spot in about 20 steps. It works, and it's what Java's TreeMap uses. But it has costs that bite in real places:

  • Code. A red-black tree insert has three repair cases and a delete has four, each with a mirror image. Few people type one from memory without a bug, and an interviewer may ask you to.
  • Many threads. Repairing a red-black tree can recolor nodes all the way up to the root and rotate subtrees. Two threads changing the tree at once can trip over each other far from where either one inserted, so the simple safe answer is one lock around the whole tree.
  • Extras. "What's my rank?" needs subtree sizes stored in every node and kept right through every rotation.

In 1990, William Pugh asked a different question: can we keep the linked list, with its cheap splicing and its plain in-order walk, and fix only the slow search? His answer, the skip list, is what this chapter builds.

In the sorted linked list of 1,000,000 scores, which part of adding a new score is slow?

Right. The splice is two pointer writes, O(1). The walk to the right spot is O(n), about 500,000 hops on average. The skip list fixes only the walk and keeps the cheap splice.

Picture a paper chain of people holding hands, sorted by height. To add someone, two people let go and grab the newcomer's hands: that's instant, and nobody else moves (that "shift everything after it" cost belongs to arrays, where items sit in numbered slots). The slow part is finding which two people: you can only start at one end and walk along the chain, hand to hand, because nobody knows where the middle is.

2One express lane

A city train line has the same problem. A local train stops at every station, so a long trip passes dozens of stops. The fix is an express train that stops only at a few big stations. To go far, you ride the express to the last big station before yours, then switch to the local for the last few stops. (Where the train picture breaks: a real express is also physically faster. Here every hop costs the same; all the saving comes from passing fewer stops.)

Do the same to the linked list. Keep the full list as the bottom lane, the local lane. Above it, build a second linked list, the express lane, that holds only every third key, and connect each express stop to the same key below it. Both lanes start at a head node, H, which holds no key.

Now the real numbers. Take 1,000,000 keys and put an express stop at every 1,000th one: the express lane has 1,000 stops. A search rides the express at most 1,000 hops, steps down, and walks at most 999 local hops before the next express stop would have taken it. That's about 2,000 hops at worst, instead of 1,000,000.

Why every 1,000th? Call the spacing k. The express lane has n/k stops and the local stretch after stepping down is under k nodes, so the worst case is about n/k + k. Make k small and the express lane gets long; make k big and the local stretch gets long:

One express lane with a stop every k nodes cuts a search to about n/k + k hops. The best spacing is k = √n, giving about 2√n: 2,000 hops for a million keys instead of a million.

1,000,000 keys, with an express stop at every 100th key. Roughly how many hops can a search take at worst?

Right. The express lane has 1,000,000 / 100 = 10,000 stops, and you may ride all of them. Then fewer than 100 local hops. 10,000 + 100 is about 10,100.

Count the two parts separately. The express lane holds one key in 100, so it has 10,000 stops, and searching for a big key rides past nearly all of them: that's the part people forget. After stepping down, fewer than 100 local hops remain. Total: about 10,100. The 2√n = 2,000 figure only holds when the spacing is exactly √n = 1,000; with spacing 100, the express lane itself is too long.

3Lanes on top of lanes

Look at what's left. A search of the million keys still rides an express lane of 1,000 stops, one stop at a time. But that express lane is itself just a sorted linked list. So it has the same problem as the original list, and the same fix: put an express lane over the express lane.

With three lanes, each keeping one key in 100 of the lane below, the top lane has 100 stops. A search rides at most 100 of them, steps down, rides fewer than 100 on the middle lane, steps down, and walks fewer than 100 at the bottom: about 300 hops. In general, with L lanes, each keeping one key in r of the lane below, r = n1/L (the L-th root of n), and a search makes at most about L × r comparisons: on each lane, up to r − 1 that say "still smaller, keep going" and one that says "too far, step down".

Push it all the way. Make every lane keep every other key of the lane below: r = 2, and there are about log₂ n lanes, 20 for a million keys. Between two neighbors on one lane there's exactly one key on the lane below, so on each lane a search takes at most one step right before stepping down. That's at most about 2 comparisons per lane, about 2 log₂ n = 40 in all, using the same "smaller or not?" comparisons as binary search. This layout is a perfect skip list:

Look at what the lanes do: L2 cuts the eight keys in half at 19, L1 cuts each half again, and L0 finishes the job. It's binary search, frozen into pointers, the same idea that gave us the BST (with one extra comparison per lane, the one that says "step down"). And the memory is modest: the lanes above the bottom hold n/2 + n/4 + n/8 + … ≈ n extra pointers, so about 2 forward pointers per key, the same as a doubly linked list.

Stack lanes: each one keeps every other key of the lane below. That gives about log₂ n lanes and at most one step right per lane, so a search costs about 2 log₂ n comparisons. A perfect skip list is binary search made of linked lists, at about 2 pointers per key.

4Inserting breaks the pattern, so let a coin decide

The perfect skip list has a rule based on position: the key at an even position is on L1, a multiple of 4 is on L2, and so on. Now insert the key 1 at the front of the list above. Every key's position goes up by one, and every key's required height changes:

All eight towers have to change. Keeping the pattern perfect means rebuilding the lanes on every insert, which is O(n): the array's shifting problem has come back in disguise. Positions are the trouble. A key's position depends on every key before it, so any rule built on positions changes whenever any key arrives.

So weaken the rule. Speed never needed exactly every other key on each lane. It needed about half the keys of each lane to continue up, spread out so there are no long gaps. Something that gives "about half, spread out" without looking at any other key is a coin. When a new key arrives, it flips a fair coin: heads, it also gets a stop on L1. Flip again: heads, it also gets L2. Keep going until the first tails. The number of lanes a key is on is its height, and it's decided once, when the key is inserted. Nothing else ever changes it.

Picture each key as a tower standing on the bottom lane, and the lanes as floors connecting towers of at least that height. Looking at the list from the side, you see a skyline: mostly one-floor huts, some two-floor houses, a few taller buildings, one tower above everything. Here is the list this chapter uses from now on, with the heights its coins gave:

import random

def random_height(rng: random.Random, p=0.5, max_level=32):
    h = 1                       # every key is on the bottom lane
    while h < max_level and rng.random() < p:
        h += 1                  # heads (probability p): one more lane
    return h                    # decided once, never changed

With a fair coin (p = 1/2), half the keys reach L1, a quarter reach L2, an eighth reach L3: the chance a key's height is at least h is (1/2)h − 1. Of 16 keys, expect 8 on L1, 4 on L2, 2 on L3 and 1 on L4, the same counts as the perfect list, just not evenly spaced. The average height is 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + … = 2, so the memory is the same too: about 2 forward pointers per key. (max_level is a safety cap on the height; section 7 says how to pick it.)

Where the skyline picture misleads: with real coins some stretches get unlucky, with a long run of huts and no express stop for a while. Section 6 shows those stretches are short on average, and why the luck can't be steered by the order of the keys.

A perfect layout depends on positions, which every insert shifts. A skip list gives each new key a random height instead: keep flipping a coin, one more lane per heads. Heights never change after insert, so an insert touches only the new key's neighbors, while the lanes still thin out by about half per level.

Why does a skip list pick a key's height with coin flips, rather than "every other key, by position"?

Right. The perfect layout is actually a little faster, and both average 2 pointers per key. The coin wins because a key's height depends on nothing but its own flips, so inserting a key never forces another key to grow or shrink.

Go back to the table above. Inserting one key at the front changed the required height of all eight keys, because each height was tied to a position, and the insert moved every position. A coin flip is tied to nothing: key 31 got height 4 when it arrived, and no later insert or delete can change that. That's the only point of the coin. It doesn't speed up searches (the perfect layout is a bit faster) and it doesn't save memory (both average 2 pointers per key). It trades a guaranteed perfect shape for a shape that's good on average and never needs repairs.

5Search, insert and delete

Every operation starts with the same walk. Stand on the head at the top lane. Look at the next node on this lane: if its key is smaller than the one you want, ride to it. If it's bigger or equal, or there's nothing there, step down a lane. Repeat until you step down off the bottom lane. Now the node right after you on the bottom lane is the key you want, or the key isn't there.

For insert and delete there's one more thing to do on the way down. A singly linked list can only splice right after a node you're holding. So at each lane, remember the last node you stood on before stepping down: it's the node that will point to the new key on that lane. Think of leaving a bookmark on every floor where you took the stairs down. Those bookmarks are traditionally called the update list.

class Node:
    def __init__(self, key, height):
        self.key = key
        self.next = [None] * height     # next[i]: the next node on lane i

def find_predecessors(head, top, key):  # top: lanes in use, at least 1
    update = [None] * top
    x = head                            # head: no key, as tall as the tallest lane
    for i in range(top - 1, -1, -1):    # from the top lane down
        while x.next[i] is not None and x.next[i].key < key:
            x = x.next[i]               # still smaller: ride this lane
        update[i] = x                   # last node before key on lane i
    return update                       # update[0].next[0] is key, if it's there

Insert: bookmarks, coins, splice

To insert 22: the walk bookmarks head on L3, 12 on L2, 12 on L1 and 19 on L0. If the node after the L0 bookmark already holds 22, stop: it's a duplicate. Otherwise flip coins for the height: heads, heads, tails gives height 3. Then, on each of the new tower's 3 lanes, splice it in right after that lane's bookmark: the new node points where the bookmark pointed, and the bookmark points to the new node. That's two pointer writes per lane, on lanes 0, 1 and 2. The L3 bookmark isn't touched; the new tower doesn't reach that high. If the new tower is taller than every existing lane, the head is the bookmark on the new lanes, since it's as tall as the tallest tower.

Nothing else changes. No node moves, no other tower grows or shrinks. At p = 1/2 the new tower is 2 lanes tall on average, so an insert writes about 4 pointers. Compare a red-black tree, whose repairs can recolor nodes all the way back up to the root.

Delete: the same walk, then unlink

To delete 31 from the list that now holds 22, walk with the key 31. The bookmarks are head on L3, 22 on L2, 25 on L1 and 25 on L0, and the node after the L0 bookmark is 31, so it's there. On each lane the 31 tower reaches, point the bookmark past it: update[i].next[i] = node.next[i]. If that leaves the top lane empty, the list gets one lane shorter. Here it does: 31 was the only tower on L3. Delete is as local as insert.

Search: ride right while the next key is smaller, else step down. Insert and delete use the same walk and remember the last node on each lane; those bookmarks are the only nodes whose pointers change. Insert splices the new tower in after them; delete points them past the old one.

In the list from section 4 (heights 3:2, 7:1, 12:3, 19:1, 25:2, 31:4, 38:1, 44:2, 50:1), you insert 40 with height 2. Whose pointers change?

Right. On L1 the search rides from 31 and sees 44, which is past 40, so it steps down and bookmarks 31. On L0 it rides 31 → 38, sees 44, and bookmarks 38. 40 is spliced in after 31 on L1 and after 38 on L0; 31's L1 link now goes to 40 instead of 44.

Walk it. L3: the head rides to 31 (31 < 40); nothing after it, step down. L2: after 31, nothing; step down. L1: after 31 comes 44, past 40: step down, bookmark 31. L0: after 31 comes 38 < 40, ride to 38; next is 44, past 40: bookmark 38. Now the bookmarks are the only nodes that change: 38 isn't on L1 at all (it has height 1), so it can't hold the L1 link. And nothing "moves right" in a linked list: the nodes after 40 keep their places, and only one pointer per lane is redirected.

6Why about 2 log₂ n, whatever order the keys arrive in

This is the part interviewers probe: "random heights could be unlucky. Why is it still O(log n)?" Split the cost into two questions: how many lanes are there, and how many steps does a search take on each lane?

How many lanes: about log₂ n

Each lane holds about half the keys of the lane below: n, n/2, n/4, … After about log₂ n halvings fewer than one key is left, so there are about log₂ n lanes: 20 for a million keys. Can one key get a freakishly tall tower? The chance a single key reaches height h is (1/2)h − 1. With a million keys, the chance that any of them reaches 31 lanes is at most 1,000,000 × (1/2)30, which is less than 1 in 1,000. For 41 lanes it's less than 1 in a million. (Adding up the chances like this, "at most n times the chance for one key", is called the union bound: it can only overestimate.) Each extra lane halves the odds.

How many steps per lane: about 2

Here's the trick that makes it easy: play the search backward, like rewinding a video. Start at the bottom, at the node where the search ended, and retrace the path toward the head at the top-left. At each node on the way back, there are only two possibilities:

  • The node is taller than the lane you're on: it also has a stop on the lane above. Then the search must have come down into this lane here (going forward, it would have been riding the higher lane over this node). So the rewind goes up one lane.
  • The node is not taller. Then the search must have arrived from the left on this lane. So the rewind steps left.

Whether a node is taller than the current lane is exactly one of that node's coin flips: heads with probability 1/2, flipped when it was inserted, with nothing to do with the key you're looking for. So the rewind is a sequence of coin flips: heads go up, tails go left. To climb one lane, you wait for one heads, and a fair coin takes 2 flips on average to come up heads. About log₂ n lanes to climb, about 2 steps each: about 2 log₂ n steps. (The rewind can only be cheaper when it reaches the head early: from there it goes straight up.)

Real numbers: for a million keys, 2 log₂ n ≈ 40. A simulation with p = 1/2 and a million keys averaged about 40 comparisons per search (between 37 and 42 across five fresh sets of coins, since each set gives a slightly different skyline). In 3,000 random searches per list, the slowest took about 60 to 75, and none took more than twice the average.

"Expected" means averaged over the coins, for every input

The randomness is in the coins, not in the data. A plain BST is fast on random input and a chain on sorted input, because its shape comes from the key order. A skip list's shape comes only from coin flips, which don't know the keys. Insert 1, 2, 3, … in order, or in reverse, or in the nastiest order you can think of: the expected cost is the same 2 log₂ n, because the coins are the same. The worst case still exists (every coin lands tails and the skip list is a plain linked list), but for a million keys its chance is (1/2)1,000,000: it won't happen.

About log₂ n lanes, and about 2 steps per lane, because rewinding the search is a coin flip per step: heads go up, tails go left. So a search costs about 2 log₂ n on average, for every input order, since the coins never see the keys.

Explain it another way: count the huts between houses

Stand on some lane, say L1, and walk right along it. Every tower on L1 has its own coin for L2: heads, it's also on L2. Starting from an L2 tower, how many L1 towers do you meet before the next L2 tower, counting that one? You're waiting for a heads, so 2 on average: about 1 hut in between, then the taller one. A search riding L1 starts at an L2 tower (where it stepped down) and compares with each next tower until one is too far. At worst it gets to the next L2 tower, which it already knew was too far. So it makes about 2 comparisons on that lane, and the same holds on every lane. Two per lane, log₂ n lanes.

Go deeper: Pugh's bound, the tail, attackers and deterministic skip lists
  • The formula. With coin probability p, Pugh's 1990 paper bounds the expected length of the search path by L(n)/p + 1/(1 − p), where L(n) = log1/p n is the expected number of lanes. For p = 1/2 and a million keys: 19.9 / 0.5 + 2 ≈ 42.
  • The tail. The cost isn't just good on average: the chance a search takes more than c times the average falls off exponentially in c, so skip list operations are O(log n) with high probability, not only in expectation.
  • Attackers. The guarantee assumes the coins are independent of the keys. If an attacker can predict your random numbers (a fixed seed, or a weak generator whose output leaks), they can delete every key that got a tall tower and keep the short ones, and the list decays toward a linked list. Seed from the operating system's randomness in anything that faces untrusted input. Hash maps have the same problem and the same fix (salted hashes).
  • No randomness at all. Deterministic skip lists (Munro, Papadakis and Sedgewick, 1992) keep between 1 and 3 nodes of each lane between neighbors on the lane above, repairing by promoting or demoting a tower. That's a 2-3-4 tree in disguise, and it brings back the repair cases the coins avoided.

A teammate inserts a million keys into your skip list in sorted order: 1, 2, 3, … What happens to the average search cost?

Right. The shape depends only on the coin flips. Sorted, reversed or shuffled input gives a list with the same random skyline, so the same expected cost.

Ask what decides the shape. In a plain BST, the key order decides it: sorted keys always go right, and you get a chain. In a skip list, a key's height comes from its own coin flips, made at insert time and unrelated to its value or arrival order. Sorted input produces the same random skyline as shuffled input. And appending at the end isn't free either: to find the end, the search still rides down from the top lane, which is the same 2 log₂ n walk (the last comparison on each lane finds nothing to the right, so it's a little cheaper, but not O(1)).

7The p knob: memory against steadiness

The coin doesn't have to be fair. Let it come up heads (go one lane higher) with probability p. Two things change. Each lane keeps a fraction p of the lane below, so there are log1/p n lanes and each key has 1/(1 − p) forward pointers on average. And the rewind now waits longer for each heads: 1/p steps per lane. So the search costs about (1/p) × log1/p n. For n = 1,000,000:

Look at 1/2 and 1/4. Half as many lanes, twice as many steps per lane: the same 40 on average. But p = 1/4 needs 1.33 pointers per key instead of 2, a third fewer. What it gives up is steadiness: fewer, longer lanes mean more luck per search. In the same million-key simulation, about 3% to 6% of searches at p = 1/4 took more than 1.5 times the average, against well under 1% at p = 1/2. Pugh's paper recommends 1/4 unless steady running times matter most. Redis uses 1/4. So does LevelDB.

The other knob is max_level, the cap on height. It exists so the head (which must be as tall as the tallest tower) has a fixed size. Pugh's rule of thumb: make it at least log1/p of the most keys you'll ever hold. LevelDB caps towers at 12 lanes, which at p = 1/4 suits up to about 412 ≈ 16.8 million keys, plenty for its in-memory buffer. Redis caps them at 32, and 432 = 264. Going past the cap breaks nothing: the top lane just holds more keys than it should, and searches slowly get longer. Both p and the cap are engineering choices, not laws.

p trades memory for steadiness. Pointers per key are 1/(1 − p); the average search is about (1/p)·log1/p n, which is the same 2 log₂ n at p = 1/2 and 1/4. So 1/4 saves a third of the pointers at the price of more variation, which is why Redis and LevelDB use it.

You switch a million-key skip list from p = 1/2 to p = 1/4. What changes?

Right. Lanes halve (20 → 10) but steps per lane double (2 → 4), so the average stays about 40. Pointers per key drop from 2 to 1.33. The cost is a wider spread of search times.

Two effects pull in opposite directions, and you have to multiply them. Fewer keys move up, so there are fewer lanes: log₄ of a million is 10, half of log₂'s 20. But each lane is sparser, so the rewind waits for a "heads" that comes 1 time in 4: 4 steps per lane instead of 2. 10 × 4 = 20 × 2 = 40. The average is unchanged; what you gain is memory (1/(1 − p) = 1.33 pointers per key instead of 2), and what you lose is steadiness.

8Ranges and ranks: what the bottom lane buys

A leaderboard asks two more questions: "list the players from score 500 to 600" (a range), and "what position am I in?" (a rank).

Ranges are a plain walk

The bottom lane is a sorted linked list of every key. So a range query is: search for the low end, O(log n), then walk the bottom lane until you pass the high end, one hop per key reported. That's O(log n + k) for k keys, with nothing to climb back up, as a tree's in-order walk must. To walk backward too (Redis's "highest scores first"), give each bottom-lane node a pointer to the previous node as well.

Ranks need one number per link

Counting your position by walking the bottom lane is O(n). The fix: on every link, write down how many bottom-lane steps it covers. Call it the link's span. A bottom-lane link always spans 1. In our list, the L3 link from the head to 31 spans 6, because 31 is the 6th key. Now ride the search path with one change: take a link when the key at its far end is smaller than or equal to the one you want (≤ instead of <), so the ride ends on the key itself rather than just before it. Add up the spans of the links you ride: the total is the key's position. Every bottom-lane step between the head and the key is covered by exactly one link on the path, so nothing is counted twice or missed.

Each link is labeled with its span. Find the position of 38 by adding the spans of the links the search rides, then find who is at position 5 by riding links while the running total stays at or under 5.

Worked numbers, for 38: ride the L3 link head → 31 (+6). On L2 and L1 the next stop is missing or past 38, so step down. On L0 ride 31 → 38 (+1). Total 7: 38 is the 7th key, and Redis's ZRANK, which counts from 0, would report 6. The reverse question, "who is 5th?", rides the same way, taking a link only if the running total plus its span stays at most 5. Both are O(log n).

Keeping spans right on insert and delete

This is the fiddly part, so do it with numbers. Insert 22 with height 3 into the list (it becomes the 5th key, right after 19). Two kinds of links change:

  • Links the new tower splits (lanes 0 to 2, below its top): each one is cut in two at 22. On L1, the link 12 → 25 spanned 2 (positions 3 to 5 before the insert). Now 12 → 22 spans 2 (positions 3 to 5) and 22 → 25 spans 1 (5 to 6). The two halves add up to the old span plus one, for the new key.
  • Links that jump over the new tower (lanes 3 and up): the L3 link head → 31 now covers one more bottom-lane node. Its span goes from 6 to 7.

To compute the split, the search records each bookmark's position as it rides down (it's the running span total at the moment it steps down). Delete is the reverse: on lanes the tower reached, merge the two halves (add them and subtract 1); on higher lanes, subtract 1 from the link that jumped over it. Still O(log n).

The bottom lane is a sorted linked list, so a range is a search plus a walk: O(log n + k). Store on each link how many bottom-lane steps it covers; the spans along the search path add up to the key's position, so rank and find-by-rank are O(log n).

In the list from section 4 (no 22), what is the position of 44, counting the smallest key as 1?

Right. Ride L3 head → 31 (+6). L2: nothing after 31, step down. L1: 31 → 44 (+2). L0: the next node is 50, past 44, so stop. 6 + 2 = 8, and 44 really is the 8th key: 3, 7, 12, 19, 25, 31, 38, 44.

You don't count links, you add up what each link covers. The L3 link head → 31 covers 6 keys of the bottom lane (3, 7, 12, 19, 25, 31). Then the L1 link 31 → 44 covers 2 more (38, 44). So the total is 6 + 2 = 8. Check by counting the sorted list: 3, 7, 12, 19, 25, 31, 38, 44. Eighth.

9Why Redis uses a skip list

Redis is an in-memory database, and its sorted set (ZSET) is the leaderboard from section 1 as a built-in type: each member (a string, like a player name) has a score (a number), and members are kept ordered by score. Members with equal scores are ordered by comparing the member strings byte by byte (alphabetical, for plain lowercase names), so the order is by the pair (score, member), and every member has one exact position.

CommandWhat it doesCostWhich part does the work
ZADD board 4200 annadd ann, or move her to a new scoreO(log N)dict to find her old entry, skip list to place her
ZSCORE board annann's scoreO(1)dict only
ZRANK board annann's position, from 0O(log N)skip list, adding spans
ZRANGE board 0 9the 10 lowestO(log N + M)find by rank, then walk the bottom lane
ZRANGEBYSCORE board 500 600everyone scoring 500 to 600O(log N + M)search for 500, then walk
ZREM board annremove annO(log N)dict, then skip list delete

So a large sorted set is two structures at once. A hash table maps member → score: that gives ZSCORE in O(1), and tells ZADD and ZREM the score they need to find a member's node. A skip list ordered by (score, member) holds the order. Each of its links stores a span for ZRANK, and each bottom-lane node also points backward, so ZREVRANGE can walk from the top score down. Its coin is p = 1/4.

Small sorted sets skip both. Up to 128 members, each at most 64 bytes, Redis stores the set as one compact flat array (a listpack, called a ziplist before Redis 7) and scans it, because for a few dozen items a scan is fast and saves all the pointers. Past either limit it converts to the dict and skip list. Both numbers are settings (zset-max-listpack-entries and zset-max-listpack-value), not laws.

Why not a balanced tree?

Redis's author, Salvatore Sanfilippo, was asked exactly this, and his answer had three parts:

  1. Memory is tunable. Lower p and the skip list needs fewer pointers per member (section 7), so it can be made lighter than tree-based alternatives. At 1/4 that's 1.33 forward pointers on average; every node of a binary tree has 2 child pointers. (Redis's own nodes also store a span beside each forward pointer and one backward pointer, so they aren't that light in practice; the point is that the knob exists.)
  2. Range reads are list walks. ZRANGE and ZREVRANGE are very common sorted set reads, and on a skip list they walk the bottom lane, which he argued is at least as cache-friendly as walking a balanced tree.
  3. It's simpler. Simpler to write, debug and extend. His example: ZRANK in O(log N) arrived as a small patch that added spans; the same feature in a red-black tree means keeping subtree sizes right through every rotation.

Notice what's not on the list: concurrency. Redis runs commands one at a time on one main thread, so it never needed the skip list's friendliness to many writers. That reason matters elsewhere, in the next section. And none of the three is a law either: a B-tree with counts, or an order-statistic tree, would also work. Redis picked the option that was cheapest to build and extend.

Go deeper: when the leaderboard outgrows one machine

A Redis sorted set is one key, and in a Redis Cluster every key lives on one node, so a single sorted set can't be bigger than one machine's memory. If it must be split, there are two common shapes, and each makes a different question cheap:

  • Split by member (hash the member name to pick a shard). Writes spread evenly. "Top 10" is exact if you take each shard's top 10 and merge them, because every member lives on exactly one shard. "What's ann's rank?" means asking every shard how many members score above her (ZCOUNT, which is O(log N) because it subtracts two ranks found with spans) and adding the answers: one round trip to every shard.
  • Split by score range (shard 1 holds scores 0 to 999, shard 2 holds 1,000 to 1,999…). Rank is cheap: the counts of the shards below, plus a local rank. But a popular score range makes one shard hot, and a member whose score changes may have to move between shards.

A big Redis sorted set is a dict plus a skip list. The dict answers "what's ann's score?" in O(1); the skip list, ordered by (score, member) with spans and a backward bottom lane, answers rank and range questions in O(log N + M). Redis chose it over a balanced tree for tunable memory, list-walk ranges and simple code, not for concurrency.

ZSCORE is O(1) but ZRANK is O(log N). Why the difference?

Right. Two structures, two jobs. The dict knows each member's score but nothing about order. The skip list knows order, and its spans turn position into an O(log N) ride instead of an O(N) count.

Think about which question needs order. "What's ann's score?" doesn't: it's a lookup by name, the hash table's job, O(1). "How many members are ahead of ann?" is about order, so it needs the skip list. Counting the members one by one would be O(N), but it doesn't have to: the spans on the links already say how many bottom-lane members each link covers, so riding the search path and adding them up takes O(log N) steps. Nothing about it is a cache.

10Many threads: concurrent maps and memtables

Now picture 16 threads inserting into one sorted structure at the same time, while other threads read it. With a red-black tree, one insert may recolor nodes up toward the root and rotate a subtree. A reader in the middle of a rotation can see a subtree that's half moved. The simple safe fix is one lock around the whole tree, and then the 16 threads take turns.

Look at a skip list insert instead, done in a careful order. First link the new node into the bottom lane, with one pointer write. At that instant the key is in the set: any search that reaches the bottom lane finds it. Then link it into L1, then L2, one pointer write each. The upper lanes are only shortcuts, so a reader who sees the node on L0 but not yet on L2 just rides a little further on a lower lane and still gets the right answer. Every intermediate state is a valid skip list. That's what makes skip lists the usual choice for sorted structures shared between threads.

Two threads splicing next to each other could still collide: both read "12 points to 25" and both try to put their node after 12. The tool for that is compare-and-swap, a processor instruction that writes a pointer only if it still holds the value you read; topic 03.09 covers it. The loser of the race sees that the pointer changed, looks again from the same bookmark, and retries. Deleting is harder: before unlinking a node, a thread marks it as deleted, so nobody splices a new node in after a node that's on its way out. Getting those details right is still delicate, but each step changes one pointer near one spot, which is what a balanced tree can't promise.

  • Java's ConcurrentSkipListMap (in java.util.concurrent since Java 6) is the standard library's sorted map for many threads. It uses compare-and-swap, not locks. Its documentation warns that size() is not constant time (it walks the list), and that ascending views and iterators are faster than descending ones, since the lanes only point forward. There is no concurrent TreeMap.
  • LevelDB and RocksDB buffer recent writes in memory in a sorted memtable, then write it to disk as a sorted file when it fills (topic 01.13 covers the whole design). LevelDB's memtable is a skip list that takes one writer at a time while readers need no lock; nodes are never removed until the whole memtable is dropped. RocksDB's default memtable is a skip list too, and it can take several writers at once.

A skip list insert is a few pointer writes near one spot, done bottom lane first. The bottom lane is the truth and upper lanes are hints, so a half-finished insert is still a valid list. That's why lock-free sorted maps (Java's ConcurrentSkipListMap) and database memtables (LevelDB, RocksDB) use skip lists.

Thread A is halfway through inserting 22: it's linked on L0 and L1, not yet on L2. Thread B searches for 22 right now. What happens?

Right. B rides L2 as if 22 weren't there, steps down, and meets 22 on L1 or L0. The missing L2 link only costs B a step or two.

Follow B's walk. On L2 it doesn't see 22, so it rides to the last node before 22 on that lane, say 12, and steps down. On L1, the next node after 12 is now 22: found. Even if 22 were only on L0, B would step down to L0 and find it there. A search never needs the upper lanes to be complete; they just make it faster. That's why the insert links the bottom lane first: the key is in the set from that one write on, and no lock is needed to keep readers correct.

11Skip list or something else?

A skip list is one of several ways to keep keys in order. Here is how it compares with the structures from earlier chapters, and with the B-tree from topic 01.13:

The honest downsides of a skip list:

  • Cache misses. Every hop follows a pointer to a node allocated somewhere else. For a million keys that's about 40 hops, and if most miss the CPU cache at about 100 ns each, a search costs a few microseconds. A B-tree packs hundreds of keys per node, so the same search touches a handful of nodes. That's why databases index disk and large memory with B-trees, and use skip lists for small, write-heavy, concurrent buffers.
  • Expected, not guaranteed. O(log n) is an average over the coins with an exponentially small tail, which is fine for almost everyone. A hard real-time system that must bound every single operation picks a structure with a worst-case guarantee.
  • Not in Python's standard library. Python has no sorted map at all. The popular sortedcontainers package uses neither a tree nor a skip list: it keeps a list of sorted Python lists, each holding roughly a thousand items (a sublist splits in two past 2,000), because in Python moving memory in bulk is cheaper than following many small objects.

Use a skip list for an in-memory sorted structure that needs ranges or ranks, simple code, or many concurrent writers. Prefer a hash map when order doesn't matter, a B-tree when data is on disk or cache misses dominate, and a balanced tree when every operation needs a worst-case bound.

You need an index over 2 billion rows on disk that answers range queries by timestamp. Which structure?

Right. On disk, the cost is reads, not comparisons. A B-tree with hundreds of keys per node reaches any of 2 billion keys in about 4 reads; a skip list's 2 log₂ n ≈ 62 hops would each be a separate read.

Count disk reads, since each one costs around 100 µs on an SSD, versus well under a microsecond for a comparison in memory. A skip list over 2 billion keys takes about 2 log₂(2 × 10⁹) ≈ 62 hops, and on disk each hop to a new node is another read. A B-tree node fills a whole disk page with hundreds of sorted keys, so 4 levels cover billions: about 4 reads, with the top levels cached. A hash map is out for a different reason: it scatters timestamps on purpose, so "everything between 9:00 and 9:05" means checking every row.

12What to say in the interview

For "how would you build a leaderboard with rank lookups?" or "why does Redis use a skip list?", a strong answer:

"A sorted linked list makes inserts cheap once you've found the spot, but finding it is O(n). A skip list adds express lanes: each key gets a random height by flipping a coin, heads meaning one more lane. Searching rides the top lane while the next key is smaller, then steps down. There are about log n lanes and about 1/p steps per lane, so it's O(log n) expected, and since the randomness comes from the coins, sorted input can't hurt it. Insert and delete change only the last node passed on each lane, so there are no rotations. For rank I'd store on each link how many bottom nodes it covers and add those up along the search path, which is what Redis does for ZRANK; a hash map next to it gives O(1) score lookups. Ranges are a search plus a walk on the bottom lane. I'd use p = 1/4 to save pointers. The trade-offs: pointer chasing means cache misses, so on disk I'd use a B-tree, and the bounds are expected rather than worst case. With many writer threads, a skip list is easier to make lock-free than a balanced tree, which is why Java's ConcurrentSkipListMap and RocksDB's memtable use one."

Habits that make you sound senior:

  • Say expected O(log n), and say what the expectation is over: the coins, not the input.
  • Explain why coins and not positions: insert would otherwise reshape everything.
  • Name the bookmarks (update list) and say only they change.
  • Bring up p as a knob: 1/2 vs 1/4, pointers vs variance.
  • Know when not to use one: disk, worst-case bounds, no need for order.

✓What to remember

  1. A skip list is a sorted linked list with express lanes on top; each lane holds about a fraction p of the lane below.
  2. One lane with spacing √n gives 2√n hops; log₂ n lanes with spacing 2 give about 2 log₂ n: binary search made of pointers.
  3. Heights come from coin flips at insert time and never change, because position-based heights would change on every insert.
  4. Search: ride right while the next key is smaller, else step down. Insert and delete change only the last node on each lane (the update list).
  5. Expected search ≈ (1/p)·log1/p n, about 2 log₂ n for p = 1/2 or 1/4, over the coins, for any input order.
  6. Pointers per key 1/(1 − p): 2 at p = 1/2, 1.33 at p = 1/4 (Redis, LevelDB), with more variation.
  7. Ranges: search plus a bottom-lane walk, O(log n + k). Rank: add up link spans, O(log n).
  8. Redis ZSET = dict (ZSCORE O(1)) + skip list (rank and ranges). Concurrent maps and memtables use skip lists because inserts are local and the bottom lane is the truth.

1. Run a skip list by hand

Keys and heights: 4:1, 9:3, 15:1, 21:2, 28:1, 33:4, 40:2. Draw the lanes. (a) Search for 28: list every comparison and say whether it rides right or steps down. (b) Insert 18 with height 2: give the bookmark on each lane and the pointers that change. (c) Then delete 33: which pointers change, and how many lanes does the list have afterward?

Answer

Lanes: L3 holds 33; L2 holds 9, 33; L1 holds 9, 21, 33, 40; L0 holds everything. (a) L3: 33 vs 28, past it: step down. L2: 9 < 28, ride to 9; 33 past: step down. L1: 21 < 28, ride to 21; 33 past: step down. L0: 28 is not < 28: step down, and the next node is 28: found. Seven comparisons, counting the final equality check. (b) L3 bookmark: head (33 is past 18). L2: head → 9 (9 < 18), then 33 is past: bookmark 9. L1: from 9, 21 is past: bookmark 9. L0: from 9, 15 < 18, ride; 21 is past: bookmark 15. Height 2, so only L0 and L1 change: 18 → 21 and 15 → 18 on L0; 18 → 21 and 9 → 18 on L1. (c) Bookmarks for 33: head on L3, 9 on L2, 21 on L1, 28 on L0. Head now points to nothing on L3, 9 → nothing on L2, 21 → 40 on L1, 28 → 40 on L0. L3 is empty, so the list drops to 3 lanes.

2. Coin arithmetic

A skip list holds n = 4,096 keys with p = 1/2. (a) How many keys do you expect on L1, L3 and L6? (b) How many forward pointers in total, on average? (c) Bound the chance that some tower reaches 23 lanes. (d) Redo (a) and (b) for p = 1/4, and give the expected number of lanes and the average search cost for both.

Answer

(a) n·pi: 2,048 on L1, 512 on L3, 64 on L6. (b) n/(1 − p) = 8,192. (c) One key reaches 23 lanes with chance (1/2)22; at most 4,096 × 2−22 = 2−10, under 1 in 1,000. (d) p = 1/4: 1,024 on L1, 64 on L3, 1 on L6; 4,096 / 0.75 ≈ 5,461 pointers. Lanes: log₂ 4,096 = 12 at p = 1/2, log₄ 4,096 = 6 at p = 1/4. Search: 2 × 12 = 24 and 4 × 6 = 24, the same on average.
