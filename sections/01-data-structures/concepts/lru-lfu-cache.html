Chapter 1 · Part 8

What to forget first

P1 a classic coding and low-level design questionabout 55 minutes10 quick checks · 9 exercises

Every fast system keeps a small copy of its slow data close by: a cache. The copy is small because fast memory is expensive, so the cache is always full, and every new item means throwing an old one out. Which one you throw out decides how often the cache saves you. "Build an LRU cache" is one of the most asked coding questions there is, because the answer combines two structures you already know, a hash map and a doubly linked list, into something neither can do alone. Its cousin, the LFU cache, is the harder follow-up. This chapter builds both from the question "who should leave?", then shows where each one fails and what real systems do instead.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Explain hit rate, and why going from 90% to 99% cuts the load behind the cache by 10×.
  2. Say what the perfect eviction choice would be, and why LRU is a guess at it.
  3. Build an LRU cache with O(1) get and put from a hash map and a doubly linked list, and say what each part is for.
  4. Show the workloads where LRU fails (scans and loops) and where LFU fails (popularity that shifts).
  5. Build an O(1) LFU cache with one list per use count, and explain why the minimum count never needs a search.
  6. Answer the follow-ups: expiry, threads, memory limits, and what Redis, Postgres, Linux and Caffeine really do.

1The problem: a small, fast box in front of a slow one

An online store has 50 million products. The product page service gets 20,000 requests a second, and every page needs the product's details. Reading them from the database takes about 5 ms, and the database can serve about 4,000 reads a second before it falls over.

20,000 is five times 4,000. So the team puts a box of RAM in front of the database. Reading from it takes about a microsecond, but it only has room for 1 million products: 2% of the catalog. Every request checks the box first, and only goes to the database if the product isn't there.

Two words first. A request that finds its item in the cache is a hit. One that doesn't is a miss, and only misses reach the database. The hit rate is the fraction of requests that hit. Now plug in numbers. With a hit rate h, the database sees 20,000 × (1 − h) reads a second, and the average request waits about (1 − h) × 5 ms, since a hit costs next to nothing:

Hit rateMisses (the miss rate)Database reads per secondAverage wait
0%100%20,000 (it falls over)5 ms
80%20%4,000 (right at its limit)1 ms
90%10%2,0000.5 ms
99%1%2000.05 ms

Look at the last two rows. The hit rate barely moved, from 90 to 99. But the database load fell from 2,000 to 200: ten times less. What the database feels is the miss rate, and that went from 10% to 1%. Every point of hit rate near the top is worth a lot.

How can 2% of the products give a hit rate anywhere near 90%? Because people don't look at products evenly. A small set of popular items gets most of the views, today's deals and bestsellers, while most of the catalog is rarely opened. If the box holds the right million, most requests hit.

But the box is always full. The moment a request misses, the product is read from the database and should go into the box, and to make room, something has to come out. Choosing what comes out is called eviction, and the rule that chooses is the eviction policy. That rule is what this chapter is about, along with the data structure that makes the rule fast: at 20,000 requests a second, the bookkeeping on every request has to cost a few steps, not a million.

A cache is a small, fast copy of slow data. Only misses reach the slow layer, so the miss rate is what matters: 90% → 99% hits is 10× less load. The cache is always full, so every miss forces an eviction, and the eviction policy decides the hit rate.

A tuning change lifts the hit rate from 90% to 99%. With 20,000 requests a second, what happens to the database?

Right. The database only sees misses. 10% of 20,000 is 2,000; 1% is 200. A 9-point change in hit rate is a 10× change in miss rate, which is why teams fight for every point near the top.

Count the requests that get through instead of the ones that are stopped. Picture a goalkeeper facing 20,000 shots. Saving 90% lets 2,000 in. Saving 99% lets 200 in. The keeper only got "9% better", but the net sees 10× fewer balls. The database is the net: its load is 20,000 × the miss rate, and the miss rate fell from 10% to 1%.

2Who leaves? Guessing the future from the past

Picture a desk with room for three binders, and an archive in the basement that takes five minutes to walk to. You need a binder that isn't on the desk. You fetch it, and one of the three on the desk has to go back down. Which one?

If someone handed you tomorrow's schedule, the answer would be easy: send back the binder you'll need latest, the one whose next use is farthest away. Every other choice brings a needed binder back sooner. László Bélády at IBM described this rule in 1966, and it gives the fewest misses possible (a proof is sketched in the "Go deeper" box below). It's called Bélády's algorithm, also MIN or OPT (for optimal).

Let's run it. The desk holds 3 binders and the requests are A B C A D B A E B A. At step 5, D arrives on a full desk holding A, B and C. Look ahead: B is next needed at step 6, A at step 7, C never again. OPT sends C back. At step 8, E arrives on a desk of A, B, D: A comes back at step 10, B at step 9, D never. D goes. Every other request hits: 5 hits out of 10, and no rule can do better on this sequence.

There's one problem: a real cache can't see the future. It only knows the past. So every practical policy is a guess about the future based on the past. The most natural guess is that the near future looks like the recent past. Something you used a minute ago is more likely to be needed again than something nobody has touched all week. This tendency is called temporal locality, and it holds for most real workloads: a user who opens a product page often refreshes it, opens it again from the cart, and so on.

Turn OPT around and use the past instead of the future. OPT evicts the item whose next use is farthest ahead. Instead, evict the item whose last use is farthest behind: the one that has gone untouched the longest. That's LRU, least recently used. Here's the same sequence under LRU, listing the desk from most to least recently used:

step  request  result   desk after (most recent first)   sent back
 1    A        miss     A
 2    B        miss     B A
 3    C        miss     C B A
 4    A        hit      A C B
 5    D        miss     D A C                            B   (unused longest)
 6    B        miss     B D A                            C
 7    A        hit      A B D
 8    E        miss     E A B                            D
 9    B        hit      B E A
10    A        hit      A B E

 LRU: 4 hits.  OPT: 5 hits.  FIFO (evict whatever arrived first): 3 hits.

LRU lost one hit to OPT, at step 5. It sent B back because B had gone unused the longest, and B was needed at the very next step. OPT knew C was never coming back. That's the price of guessing. The third rule in the last line, FIFO (first in, first out), evicts the item that arrived first, whether or not it's been used since. At step 5 it would evict A, even though A was just used at step 4. Ignoring use completely costs it another hit.

The perfect rule (Bélády's OPT) evicts the item needed farthest in the future. Nobody knows the future, so real policies guess from the past. LRU guesses that the item unused for the longest time is the one needed latest. It's a good guess whenever recent use predicts future use.

An LRU cache holds 3 items. The requests are A B C A D. Which item is evicted when D arrives?

Right. After step 4 the order of last use is A (step 4), C (step 3), B (step 2). B has gone unused the longest. The re-read of A at step 4 is exactly what saves it.

LRU doesn't care when an item arrived; it cares when it was last used. Write the last-use time next to each item when D arrives: A was used at step 4 (it arrived at step 1, but was read again), B at step 2, C at step 3. The smallest last-use time is B's. Evicting A would be FIFO, which forgets that A was just read. Evicting C throws out an item that was used more recently than B.

Go deeper: why OPT is optimal, and Bélády's anomaly

Why farthest-next-use is best, in one exchange argument. Take any rule that, at some miss, evicts X, while OPT would evict Y, whose next use is later than X's. Build a second rule that evicts Y instead and from then on copies the first rule's moves, swapping the roles of X and Y. The two desks differ only in X vs Y. If the first rule evicts Y before X comes back, the second rule evicts X at that moment, and from then on the desks are identical. Otherwise X is requested first: the first rule misses and the second rule hits. Either way the second rule never has more misses. Do this at every miss, one at a time, and you turn any rule into OPT without adding a single miss.

More room can mean more misses, for FIFO. Run the requests 1 2 3 4 1 2 5 1 2 3 4 5 through FIFO. With 3 slots you get 3 hits. With 4 slots you get only 2. Bigger cache, fewer hits. That's Bélády's anomaly (1969). LRU and OPT can never do this: an LRU cache of size k always holds exactly the k most recently used items, so a cache of size k + 1 holds everything the smaller one holds, plus one. Policies with that property are called stack algorithms, and for them more memory never hurts.

Where OPT is used for real: as a yardstick. You record a real request log, replay it through OPT offline, and compare. If LRU gets 85% and OPT gets 88%, a smarter policy can win at most 3 points, and your time is better spent on a bigger cache.

3The obvious designs, and why each one is slow

We know the rule. Now we need a structure that runs it fast. Every request is a get(key), and every miss is followed by a put(key, value) that may evict. With 1 million entries and 20,000 requests a second, each operation should cost a handful of steps. Let's try the obvious designs and see where each one breaks.

Attempt 1: a hash map with timestamps

Keep a dict from key to (value, last_used), where last_used is a counter that goes up by one on every request. Finding a key is O(1). Marking it as used is O(1): overwrite the counter. Evicting is the problem:

cache = {}                     # key -> (value, last_used)
tick = 0

def touch(key):
    global tick
    tick += 1
    value, _ = cache[key]
    cache[key] = (value, tick)   # O(1): stamp the time

def evict():
    oldest = min(cache, key=lambda k: cache[k][1])   # looks at all n entries
    del cache[oldest]

The dict has no idea which entry is oldest, so min looks at all of them. At a 90% hit rate the service has 2,000 misses a second, each scanning 1 million entries: 2 billion steps a second, just for bookkeeping.

Attempt 2: a list in order of use

Keep the keys in a Python list, least recent at the front. Eviction is order.pop(0), and a use moves the key to the back. But a Python list is an array. pop(0) shifts every remaining item one slot left, and finding the key to move (order.remove(key)) walks the list until it finds it. Both are O(n): about half a million steps per request, on average. Worse than attempt 1, because now even hits are slow.

Attempt 3: a heap of timestamps

The least recently used entry is the one with the smallest last_used, and "give me the smallest" is what a min-heap does. The catch is that every hit changes a key's timestamp, and a heap can't change an item's priority in place. The fix from the heaps chapter is lazy deletion: push a fresh (tick, key) on every use and skip stale entries when they surface at the top. That works, at O(log n) amortized per operation: about 20 steps for a million entries, plus a heap that fills up with stale entries, one per hit, until you rebuild it. Not bad. But can we get to O(1)?

What do we actually need?

Look at how the recency order changes. It never needs a real sort. Only three things ever happen to it:

  1. A new key arrives and becomes the newest.
  2. An existing key is used: it's pulled out from wherever it is and becomes the newest.
  3. The oldest key is removed.

So the order behaves like a line at a counter: people join at one end and leave from the other, except that someone in the middle can step out and rejoin at the back. A line where anyone can step out without the others shuffling forward one by one is a linked list. And "pulled out from wherever it is" needs us to find that person in the line fast, which is what a hash map does.

DesignFind a keyMark it newestEvict the oldest
Hash map with timestampsO(1)O(1)O(n) scan
List in order of useO(n) walkO(n) shiftO(n) shift
Heap of timestamps (lazy)O(1) with a dictO(log n)O(log n) amortized
Hash map + doubly linked listO(1)O(1)O(1)

The recency order only ever changes in three ways: add at the newest end, remove from the oldest end, and move one item from anywhere to the newest end. A linked list does all three in O(1), as long as something can hand it the right node. A hash map hands it the node.

A teammate proposes: "Keep a dict of key → last-used time. Hits are O(1). On eviction, call min() over the dict." For a 1-million-entry cache with 2,000 misses a second, what's wrong with it?

Right. Hits are cheap, but the dict keeps no order, so finding the oldest means looking at everyone. 2,000 evictions × 1,000,000 entries = 2 × 109 steps a second. The fix keeps the entries in recency order all the time, so the oldest is always at one end.

A dict is O(1) for "give me the entry for this key". Eviction asks a different question: "which entry has the smallest timestamp?" The dict has no idea; it would have to look at every value. Picture a coat check that stores coats by ticket number: finding coat 4,712 is instant, but "which coat has been here longest?" means walking every rack. With a million coats and 2,000 such questions a second, that's 2 billion looks a second. (Ties can't happen here, since the counter goes up by one on every use.)

4Two structures, each covering the other's weakness

Picture a pile of magazines on a coffee table. Every time you read one, you pull it out of the pile and put it on top. You never sort anything, yet the pile is always in order of use: the top is the one you read last, and the bottom is the one you haven't touched in the longest time. When the table is full and a new magazine arrives, the bottom one goes in the recycling.

Pulling a magazine out of the middle is easy on a real table: the ones above it drop down to close the gap. That's what a doubly linked list gives us. Each entry is a node that knows its neighbor on each side (prev and next). To pull a node out, its two neighbors point at each other: two pointer writes, no shifting, whether the list holds 10 entries or 10 million. Why doubly linked? With only next pointers, a node can't reach the node in front of it, and that node's next is what has to change. Finding it means walking from the head: O(n) again. The prev pointer is what makes "pull it out from wherever it is" O(1). A Python list (an array) can't do this; it has to shift everything after the gap.

Where the coffee table picture breaks: you can see the magazine you want in the pile. A computer can't. Holding only the list, finding key "A" means walking from the top. So we add an index: a hash map from each key to its node. The map answers "where is A?" in O(1); the list answers "who is oldest?" and "move A to the top" in O(1). Each covers exactly what the other can't do.

 hash map:    "D" ─┐            "A" ─┐            "C" ─┐
                   ▼                 ▼                 ▼
 [HEAD] ◄──► [ D | 4 ] ◄──► [ A | 1 ] ◄──► [ C | 3 ] ◄──► [TAIL]
             most recent                     least recent

 Each node holds its key, its value, and prev / next pointers.
 HEAD and TAIL are sentinels: dummy nodes that hold no data.

The two sentinels are the trick from the linked-list chapter. They are permanent dummy nodes, one at each end. Every real node always has a real node or a sentinel on both sides, so "pull a node out" and "put a node after HEAD" never need an if for the first node, the last node or an empty list. The most recent entry is HEAD.next; the least recent is TAIL.prev. An empty cache is HEAD and TAIL pointing at each other.

Step through a cache of capacity 3. Watch two things: the map lookup that finds a node without walking the list, and the node that falls off the TAIL end when a new key arrives on a full cache.

An LRU cache of capacity 3. Top row: the hash map (key → node). Bottom row: the doubly linked list, most recent next to HEAD. Yellow: found by a map lookup. Blue: just moved or inserted. Red: evicted. Step with ▶| and read the caption at every step.

Here are the only two list moves the whole cache needs. Each is a few pointer writes with no loop:

class Node:
    __slots__ = ("key", "value", "prev", "next")
    def __init__(self, key=None, value=None):
        self.key, self.value = key, value   # the key too: eviction needs it
        self.prev = self.next = None

def unlink(node):              # O(1): the neighbors point past it
    node.prev.next = node.next
    node.next.prev = node.prev

def push_front(head, node):    # O(1): right after the HEAD sentinel
    node.prev, node.next = head, head.next
    head.next.prev = node      # the old first node points back at us
    head.next = node

Mind the order in push_front: read head.next (the old first node) before you overwrite it. Moving a node to the front is unlink then push_front: six pointer writes in total, no matter how long the list is.

An LRU cache is a hash map from key to node, plus a doubly linked list of those nodes in order of use, with sentinels at both ends. The map finds any node in O(1). The list moves a node to the front, and removes the oldest from the back, in O(1).

The cache is full, so it unlinks the node before TAIL. It must also delete that entry from the hash map. How does it know which key to delete?

Right. That's why each node holds the key as well as the value: the list knows which node is oldest, but only the key can remove it from the map. del cache[node.key], O(1).

Picture a coat check where the coats hang in order of arrival, and a ticket book maps ticket numbers to hooks. To clear the oldest coat, you take it off the last hook, but you also have to tear out its ticket, or someone can later hand in that ticket and be pointed at an empty hook. If the coat has no tag with its ticket number, you'd have to search the whole book: O(n). So every coat carries its ticket number. In code, unlinking a node changes only the prev/next pointers; the dict still maps the key to the detached node until you delete it with node.key. Forget that, and a later get "hits" a value that was evicted.

Explain it another way: a library with a returns cart

A small library branch has one shelf with room for 100 books, ordered by when each was last borrowed: the most recent at the left end. The catalog card for each book says exactly which slot it's in (the hash map). When someone borrows a book, the librarian looks up its card, pulls the book out, and puts it at the left end; the books to its right stay where they are, because the shelf is really a chain of book-ends that can open anywhere (the linked list). When a new book arrives and the shelf is full, the rightmost book goes to the warehouse, and its catalog card is thrown away, which is why each book has its title on the spine (the key in the node).

5get and put, and the details that bite

With the two list moves, both operations are a few lines. In words:

get(key):
  1. node = map.get(key). Not there?  return the default. Change nothing.
  2. unlink(node), push_front(node)      it's now the most recent
  3. return node.value

put(key, value):
  1. key already in the map?  update node.value, move it to the front, done.
                              (an update never evicts anything)
  2. map is full?             lru = TAIL.prev
                              unlink(lru); delete map[lru.key]
  3. node = Node(key, value); map[key] = node; push_front(node)

Every step is O(1): dict operations are O(1) on average, and the list moves are a fixed number of pointer writes. Memory is O(capacity): one map slot and one node per entry. The node's two pointers are the price of the O(1) moves: 16 bytes per entry with 8-byte pointers. In Python a node with four slots is about 64 bytes on its own.

That's the whole algorithm. Interviewers rarely fail people on the idea. They fail them on these details, so say each one out loud as you code:

  • An update is not an insert. If the key is already there, change the value and move it to the front. Evicting first would throw out an innocent entry and leave the cache one short.
  • Evict both halves. Unlink the node and delete its map entry. A map entry pointing at a detached node is a memory leak and a ghost: later gets "hit" a value that's no longer in the order.
  • A miss changes nothing. get on a missing key returns the default. It doesn't evict and doesn't reorder.
  • Evict before or after inserting, but be consistent. "Full? evict, then insert" and "insert, then evict if over capacity" both work. Mixing them gives an off-by-one capacity.
  • Capacity 1 and the empty cache. With sentinels these need no special code. Without them they're where the bugs hide: the node you evict is also the head, or the tail, or both.
  • A None value is still a hit. Don't write if cache.get(k): to test presence. Test the map.

An LRU cache of capacity 2 holds A and B (B used last). You call put("A", new_value). What gets evicted?

Right. A is already in the cache, so this is an update: change its value and move it to the front. The cache still holds 2 entries, A (most recent) and B. B would only be evicted by the next new key.

Count the entries after the call. A was there before and is there after; nothing new arrived, so the count is still 2, which fits. Eviction exists only to make room, and an update needs no room. Treating the update as "remove A, insert A" works only if you remove A first; checking "is the cache full?" before noticing that A is already in it evicts B for nothing.

Go deeper: is it really O(1)?

The list moves are O(1) in the worst case: a fixed number of pointer writes. The hash map is O(1) on average, as the hash-maps chapter showed: a bad hash or an attacker who picks colliding keys can make lookups slow, and a resize occasionally costs O(n), spread over many inserts. So the honest answer is "O(1) average per operation, O(capacity) memory". A cache facing untrusted keys should use a hash with a random seed (Python does this for strings) or a structure with a guaranteed bound.

On real hardware the cost is also memory traffic. Every get writes pointers in up to five nodes (the node, its two old neighbors, the HEAD sentinel and the old first node), scattered around memory. For a CPU cache, or an operating system tracking millions of memory pages, that's far too slow, which is why hardware and kernels approximate LRU instead (section 10).

6Python already has one

In real Python code you'd rarely build the list yourself. Two tools in the standard library already are a hash map plus a doubly linked list:

from collections import OrderedDict
od = OrderedDict()
od["a"] = 1; od["b"] = 2; od["c"] = 3
od.move_to_end("a")          # O(1): order is now b, c, a
od.popitem(last=False)       # O(1): removes ("b", 2), the oldest

from functools import lru_cache
@lru_cache(maxsize=1000)     # the default maxsize is 128
def load_product(product_id):
    ...                      # repeat calls with the same id skip the body

OrderedDict remembers the order keys were added and lets you move a key to either end. In CPython it's a dict plus a doubly linked list of its keys, the same structure as section 4. functools.lru_cache wraps a function: its arguments become the key, the return value the value, and its C implementation is a dict plus a circular doubly linked list with one sentinel. Call load_product.cache_info() to see its hits and misses. In Java, LinkedHashMap built with accessOrder = true, plus an override of removeEldestEntry, is an LRU cache in a few lines.

In an interview, ask whether you may use them. The usual answer to "build an LRU cache" is "build the list yourself", because the list is the point. Knowing that OrderedDict is the same idea is still worth a sentence: it shows you know where the structure lives in real code.

OrderedDict (move_to_end, popitem(last=False)) and functools.lru_cache are the same hash map + doubly linked list. Use them at work; expect to build the list yourself in an interview.

You build an LRU cache on an OrderedDict, newest at the end. Which two calls do a hit and an eviction?

Right. A hit moves the key to the newest end; eviction pops from the oldest end. last=False means "from the front", which is where the oldest key sits.

Line up the two ends: the newest key is at the end (that's where move_to_end puts it), so the oldest is at the front. Plain popitem() pops from the end, so it would evict the key you just used, the opposite of LRU. And assigning to an existing key keeps its position: in any Python dict, updating a value doesn't move the key, so od[key] = od[key] doesn't record the use at all.

Go deeper: why not a plain dict?

Since Python 3.7 a plain dict keeps insertion order too, so it's tempting to fake LRU with one: on a hit, value = d.pop(key); d[key] = value moves the key to the end, and del d[next(iter(d))] evicts the oldest. It gives correct answers. But in CPython, deleting from the front of a dict leaves an empty slot at the front of its internal entry array, and next(iter(d)) has to step over every such slot until the dict next resizes and compacts. In one measurement, 300,000 inserts into a full 100,000-entry "dict LRU" took about 7 seconds, over 100× longer than a linked list. OrderedDict doesn't have this problem: its own linked list knows where the oldest key is. The lru_cache exercise has a test for exactly this.

7When recency lies: scans and loops

LRU rests on one guess: recently used means soon used again. Two common access patterns break that guess badly.

The scan. At 2 a.m. a reporting job reads every one of the 50 million products, once each. Under LRU, each one-off read becomes the most recent entry, so it pushes a popular product out of the cache. After a million of them, the cache is full of products nobody will look at again, and every popular product has been evicted. In the morning the hit rate collapses, and the database takes most of the 20,000 reads a second until the cache warms back up. One job that read each item once wiped out a cache full of items read thousands of times.

The loop. A job repeatedly walks the same 1,000,001 items, with a cache of 1,000,000. When item 1,000,001 arrives, the least recent entry is item 1, so item 1 is evicted. Then item 1 is requested again: miss, and it evicts item 2, which is requested next: miss. Every request evicts exactly the item that will be needed soonest. LRU gets zero hits, the worst possible, while OPT would keep most of the loop and hit on almost every request.

On the scan, even OPT tops out at 68%: the 150 scanned keys are each requested once, so each one is a miss no policy can avoid. (A miss on an item's first request is called a compulsory miss.) LRU loses another 16 points because the scan keys push the 8 hot keys out. LFU doesn't: a scanned key has been used once, while every hot key has been used many times, so the scan keys evict each other and the hot keys stay. On the loop, nothing but OPT gets a single hit until the cache holds all 15 keys. The "Zipf" pattern is what real popularity usually looks like: a few keys get most of the requests, and the request rate falls off steeply down the list. There, remembering how often beats remembering only how recently.

LRU fails when recent use doesn't predict future use. A one-time scan flushes the hot items out, and a loop just bigger than the cache gets zero hits. Counting how often each key is used protects frequently used keys from one-off traffic. That's LFU, next.

A function decorated with @lru_cache(maxsize=100) is called in a loop with arguments 0, 1, 2, …, 100, then 0, 1, …, 100 again, many times. What's the hit rate?

Right. There are 101 distinct arguments and 100 slots. When 100 is called, the least recent is 0, so 0 is evicted, and 0 is exactly what's called next. Its insert evicts 1, which is called next, and so on forever. Raising maxsize to 101 takes the hit rate to nearly 100% in one step.

Trace the first few calls after the cache fills. It holds 0 to 99. Call 100: miss, and LRU evicts the least recently used, 0. Next call: 0. Miss, so evict the least recent, which is now 1. Next call: 1. Miss. Each miss throws out exactly the key needed next, so every call misses. "Only one doesn't fit" sounds like 1 miss per lap, but with LRU the one that doesn't fit keeps changing: it's always the next one. OPT would evict the key needed farthest ahead and miss only about once per lap.

8LFU: count uses, not just time

The scan hurt LRU because LRU forgets everything about a key except its last use. A product read 40,000 times yesterday and a product read once by a reporting job look the same if their last reads were at the same moment. So keep one more number per key: how many times it has been used. Then evict the key used the fewest times. That's LFU, least frequently used.

Picture a small library deciding which books stay on the front shelf: the ones with the most checkouts. A book that one class borrowed once for a project doesn't push out a book that's borrowed every week.

Two keys can tie on the count, and the rule needs a tie-break. The usual one is recency: among the keys with the fewest uses, evict the least recently used. That's what "LFU" means in the classic interview problem, and in the playground above. Work through it with capacity 2:

put a        a:1
put b        a:1  b:1
get a        a:2  b:1
put c        full. Fewest uses: b (1). Evict b.        a:2  c:1
get c        a:2  c:2            (tie, and a is now the less recent)
put d        full. a and c tie at 2; a is less recent. Evict a.   c:2  d:1

First try: a heap

"Evict the smallest (count, last use)" sounds like a min-heap keyed by (count, last_used). The trouble is the same as attempt 3 in section 3: every hit changes a key's count, and a heap can't change a priority in place. With lazy deletion (push a new entry, skip stale ones at the top) it works at O(log n) amortized per operation, and the heap fills with stale entries, one per hit, until you rebuild it. Fine for a first answer. The interviewer will then ask for O(1), and the way there comes from one small fact about how counts change.

LFU evicts the key with the fewest uses, breaking ties by least recent use. A heap of (count, last use) does it in O(log n) with lazy deletion; O(1) needs a structure built around how counts change.

An LFU cache of capacity 2: put a, put b, get b, get a, put c. Which key is evicted?

Right. a and b both have 2 uses, so the tie-break decides: b was last used at step 3, a at step 4. b is the less recent. c then goes in with 1 use.

Write each key's count and last use when c arrives: a has 2 uses (put at step 1, get at step 4), b has 2 uses (put at step 2, get at step 3). Same count, so the tie-break looks at last use, not first insert: b's last use (step 3) is older than a's (step 4). Evicting a would be a FIFO tie-break. And eviction happens before c goes in, to make room: c isn't a candidate. (Whether a newcomer should be let in at all is a real question, called admission. Section 10 comes back to it.)

9LFU in O(1): one list per use count

Here's the small fact. A key's count only ever changes by +1, a new key always starts at 1, and an evicted key leaves completely. Counts never jump, never go down. That's what lets us drop the heap.

Picture a hotel where the floor number is a guest's use count. A new guest checks in on floor 1. Every time a guest is used, they move up exactly one floor. On each floor the guests stand in a line, most recently used at the front. To evict, go to the lowest occupied floor and take the guest at the back of its line: fewest uses, and among those the least recent. Each floor's line is the LRU list from section 4.

 map:  key → node (value, count)

 counts:  1 │ [HEAD] ⇄ e ⇄ [TAIL]          ← min_count = 1
          2 │ [HEAD] ⇄ c ⇄ b ⇄ [TAIL]
          3 │ [HEAD] ⇄ a ⇄ [TAIL]
                     most      least
                    recent     recent

 evict:  the node at the back of the list for min_count
 a use:  unlink from list k, push to the front of list k + 1

So the cache has three parts: a map from key to node (each node holds its key, value and count), a map from each count to a doubly linked list of the nodes with that count, and one integer, min_count, the lowest floor that has anyone on it. A use is two O(1) list moves. An eviction is "back of list min_count", also O(1), if we always know min_count without looking.

Why min_count never needs a search

This is the part people get stuck on, and the part interviewers probe. You might expect that when the lowest floor empties out, you'd have to climb floors until you find someone. You never do. Look at every way min_count can change:

  1. A new key arrives. It goes on floor 1, and no floor is lower than 1. So min_count = 1. No search.
  2. A key on floor min_count is used and was the last one there. It moves up to floor min_count + 1, so that floor now has someone, and nothing was below min_count before. So the new minimum is exactly min_count + 1. No search. (If the floor still has other guests, or the key used was on a higher floor, the minimum doesn't change.)
  3. An eviction empties floor min_count. Now we really don't know the next occupied floor. But look at when evictions happen: only inside put of a new key, right before that key is inserted on floor 1. Case 1 sets min_count = 1 a moment later, so the unknown value is never read.

A use touches two floors, an eviction touches one, an insert sets the minimum to 1. Every operation is O(1). The idea of one bucket per use count was published as "An O(1) algorithm for implementing the LFU cache eviction scheme" (Shah, Mitra and Matani, 2010), and it's what interviewers mean by "LFU in O(1)".

An O(1) LFU cache of capacity 3. One row per use count, most recent at the left of each row. Blue: the key just used or inserted, shown in its new row. Red: the key about to be evicted. Watch the min_count marker: it moves up by exactly one when its row empties, and drops to 1 whenever a new key arrives.

The heart of it is the move on every hit. With a doubly linked list class of your own for each count (it needs remove, push_front and a length), it's this:

def touch(self, node):                  # one more use of an existing key
    k = node.count
    self.lists[k].remove(node)          # O(1) unlink, as in section 4
    if len(self.lists[k]) == 0:
        del self.lists[k]               # don't keep empty floors around
        if k == self.min_count:
            self.min_count = k + 1      # case 2: the lowest floor emptied
    node.count = k + 1
    self.lists.setdefault(k + 1, DList()).push_front(node)

O(1) LFU: a map from key to node, a map from count to a doubly linked list (most recent first), and min_count. A use moves a node from list k to the front of list k + 1. Evict from the back of list min_count. min_count never needs a search: a new key resets it to 1, and a use can only raise it by exactly 1.

An eviction can leave the lowest-count list empty. Why doesn't the O(1) LFU cache have to search upward for the next non-empty list?

Right. Eviction exists only to make room for a new key, and the new key's count is 1, the lowest possible. So the minimum after any put of a new key is 1, whatever the eviction emptied.

Look at when the cache evicts: only inside put, only for a brand-new key, only right before inserting it. The new key has 1 use, and no count can be lower, so min_count becomes 1. Whatever the eviction emptied doesn't matter. "The next list up is non-empty" is true after a use (the used key just moved there), but not after an eviction: if the counts were 1 and 7, evicting the only key with 1 leaves list 2 empty. And there's no heap at all; that was the O(log n) design this one replaces.

10LFU's own flaws, and what real systems do

LFU fixed the scan. It has two flaws of its own, and the playground showed the first one.

  • It never forgets. In "Popularity shifts", the first 8 keys pile up dozens of uses each, then nobody asks for them again. Their counts stay high forever, so they sit in the cache while the new popular keys fight over the slots that are left. At cache size 10, LFU gets 63% where LRU gets 97%. Think of a news site where last week's big story, with 50,000 uses, never leaves.
  • Newcomers are first in line to go. A new key starts at 1, the lowest count there is, so it's first in line for the next eviction, along with every other key that has only 1 use, unless it gets used again before then. A key that will be very popular can be thrown out before it has the chance to show it.

Real systems don't run pure LRU or pure LFU. They blend recency and frequency, forget old counts, and approximate to save memory and locking. The main tricks:

  • Aging: make old counts fade. Halve every count once in a while, or decay a key's count based on how long it's been idle. Yesterday's 50,000 becomes 25,000, then 12,500, and eventually a new story can beat it.
  • Two segments (2Q, segmented LRU): a new key enters a small probation list. Only a second use promotes it to the main, protected list. A scan touches each key once, so it only churns probation, and the protected keys survive. ARC (adaptive replacement cache, from IBM, used by ZFS) goes one step further: it keeps a recency list and a frequency list, remembers the keys it recently evicted from each, and shifts room toward whichever list those "ghost" keys show was too small.
  • Admission: before letting a newcomer in, compare its estimated popularity with the key it would evict, and keep the better one. The estimate comes from a compact count of recent requests (a Count-Min Sketch, topic 01.12).
  • Approximate LRU: moving a node on every read costs six pointer writes and, with threads, a lock. CLOCK replaces the list with one bit per entry:
  entries in a circle, each with a "used" bit:

         hand
          ▼
   [A 1] [B 0] [C 1] [D 1] [E 0]  → back to A

  a hit:    set the entry's bit to 1        (no list moves at all)
  evict:    look at the entry under the hand
              bit 1 → clear it to 0 (a second chance), move the hand on
              bit 0 → evict this one
  Here: B has bit 0, so the hand clears nothing and evicts B.

An entry used since the hand last passed gets a second chance; one that wasn't is evicted. That's roughly "not used recently", for one bit per entry and nothing to update on a hit except the bit. Operating systems have used CLOCK and its variants for page replacement for decades.

LFU never forgets old popularity and evicts newcomers too quickly. Real caches blend the two signals and approximate: aging counts, a probation segment against scans, an admission filter for newcomers, and CLOCK bits or sampling instead of a list move on every read.

A news site uses a pure LFU cache with no aging. Last week's stories have about 50,000 uses each and fill 90% of the cache. A big story breaks today. What goes wrong?

Right. Their counts never drop, so nothing new can outrank them. Every new story starts at 1 and competes only with the other new stories for the leftover slots. The fix is aging: halve or decay the counts over time, so last week's 50,000 shrinks and today's story can win.

Follow one new story. It enters with 1 use, the lowest count in the cache, so it's the first candidate for eviction. Even if it survives and climbs to 1,000 uses, it's still far below 50,000, so it can never displace an old story; only other new stories can be evicted to make room for it. LFU has no idea how old a use is: "oldest" is an LRU idea, and LFU ignores it except to break ties. So the old stories keep 90% of the cache forever, unless counts age.

11The follow-ups: expiry, threads, memory, many machines

After "build an LRU cache", the follow-ups are predictable. Each one has a short, solid answer.

"Entries should expire after 60 seconds"

Expiry and eviction answer different questions. Eviction asks "who leaves to make room?". Expiry asks "is this copy too old to trust?", because the database may have changed since. A TTL (time to live) stamps each entry with an expiry time. Two ways to remove expired entries, and real caches use both:

  • Lazily: on every get, check the entry's expiry; if it's past, delete it and report a miss. Cheap, but an expired key nobody reads stays in memory forever.
  • Actively: a background sweep removes expired entries. Redis does both: a key is checked when it's read, and a background job repeatedly checks a small batch of the keys that have a TTL and deletes the expired ones.

If every entry gets the same TTL, the order in which entries expire is the order in which they were last written. That's a second linked list, ordered by write time, so the next entry to expire is always at its front: O(1) to find. The least recently used entry and the next entry to expire are generally different (an old entry that was just read is recent but about to expire), so each node sits in both lists. With a different TTL per entry, expiry order is a priority queue: a heap, or a timing wheel (the heaps chapter). Exercise 6 builds the same-TTL version.

"Make it thread-safe"

Here's the catch: in an LRU cache every read is a write. get moves a node, which rewrites pointers in up to five nodes. Two threads doing it at once can corrupt the list, so even reads need the lock, and a read-write lock doesn't help (topic 03.03). One lock around the whole cache is correct and is the right first answer. At high request rates it becomes the bottleneck, and the standard fixes are:

  • Shard it: split the cache into 16 or 64 independent LRU caches, picked by a hash of the key, each with its own lock. Threads only collide when they hit the same shard. The price: eviction is LRU within a shard, so the global order is only approximate.
  • Don't move on read: use CLOCK bits or Redis-style sampling, so a hit only sets a bit or a timestamp.
  • Buffer the reads: record hits in a small buffer and apply the list moves in batches under the lock. Caffeine does this.

"Cap it at 500 MB, not 1 million entries"

Entries have different sizes. Keep a running total of bytes; on put, evict from the LRU end until the new entry fits. One put may evict several entries, and an entry bigger than the whole budget shouldn't be cached at all.

"The cache has to run on 20 machines"

Split the keys across machines by a hash of the key, so each key has one home (consistent hashing, topic 02.15), and run an LRU cache on each machine. Then the interesting problems are about the slow layer behind the cache: when a hot key expires, a thousand requests can miss at the same moment and all hit the database (a cache stampede). Fixes include letting one request reload the key while the others wait for it, and adding random jitter to TTLs so keys don't all expire together (topics 04.05 and 03.14).

Expiry is about freshness, eviction about room: use both, removing expired entries lazily on read and actively in the background. In an LRU cache every get is a write, so it needs a lock; shard it, or stop moving nodes on reads, when that lock gets hot. Real caches budget bytes, not entries.

12What to say in the interview

For "design an LRU cache with O(1) get and put", a strong answer:

"I need three operations in O(1): find a key, mark it most recently used, and remove the least recently used. A hash map finds keys but has no order. A doubly linked list keeps the order and moves or removes a node in O(1), but can't find one. So I combine them: the map goes from key to list node, and the list runs from most recent at the head to least recent at the tail, with sentinel nodes so there are no edge cases. A get looks up the node and moves it to the front. A put updates and moves an existing key, or, if the cache is full, unlinks the tail node and deletes its key from the map, which is why each node stores its key, then inserts the new node at the front. Everything is O(1) on average, with O(capacity) memory. LRU's weak spot is scans and loops: a one-off scan flushes the hot keys. If that matters I'd use LFU, which in O(1) needs a list per use count and a min-count pointer, or a segmented LRU like the ones databases use. In production I'd also think about TTLs, and about locking, since every get is a write."

  1. Clarify: capacity in entries or bytes? Does put on an existing key count as a use? Threads? Expiry?
  2. Build the design from the operations: say what each structure gives you and what it can't do.
  3. Code the two list moves first (unlink, push_front), then get and put on top of them.
  4. Say the traps out loud: the key in the node, update vs insert, delete from both structures, sentinels.
  5. Close with trade-offs: where LRU fails, LFU and its O(1) trick, what real systems approximate, and thread safety.

✓What to remember

  1. Only misses reach the slow layer: 90% → 99% hits is 10× less load. The eviction policy decides the hit rate.
  2. The perfect policy (Bélády's OPT) evicts the item needed farthest in the future. LRU guesses it from the past: evict the item unused the longest.
  3. LRU = hash map (key → node) + doubly linked list in recency order, with sentinels. get, put and evict are O(1) average.
  4. Each node stores its key, so eviction can delete it from the map. An update never evicts. A miss changes nothing.
  5. OrderedDict and functools.lru_cache are the same structure. A plain dict with next(iter(d)) is a trap in CPython.
  6. LRU fails on scans (they flush hot keys) and on loops just bigger than the cache (0 hits).
  7. LFU evicts the fewest uses, ties by recency. O(1): a list per count plus min_count, which a new key resets to 1 and a use raises by at most 1.
  8. LFU never forgets and is hard on newcomers. Real systems blend and approximate: aging, probation segments, admission filters, CLOCK, sampling.
  9. Follow-ups: TTL (lazy plus active removal; same TTL means expiry order is write order), every get is a write so locking is needed (sharding helps), byte budgets, stampedes.

2. The same requests through LFU

Same capacity and the same ten operations, now with LFU (ties: least recently used). After each step, draw the lists per use count (most recent first) and write min_count. Which evictions differ from exercise 1, and why?

3. Pick the policy

For each, say LRU, LFU (with aging?) or something else, and why: (a) a database buffer pool where analysts run full-table scans every night; (b) a video service where a few hundred titles stay popular for months; (c) a news site where popularity changes every hour; (d) a batch job that loops over 1.2 million records with a 1-million-entry cache; (e) a cache for user sessions that must disappear 30 minutes after the last write.

Answer

(a) Scan-resistant: segmented LRU or midpoint insertion, as Postgres and InnoDB do, or LFU with aging. Plain LRU would be flushed every night. (b) LFU (with slow aging): popularity is stable, and frequency protects the hits from one-off views. (c) LRU, or LFU with fast aging: plain LFU keeps old stories forever. (d) No recency policy helps: LRU, FIFO and LFU all get about 0 hits on a loop bigger than the cache. Make the cache hold the whole loop, or pin a fixed 1 million records and let the rest miss (that gets about 1/1.2 ≈ 83% hits). (e) A TTL decides correctness here; pair it with LRU for space. Sessions all get the same TTL, so the expiry order is the write order.
