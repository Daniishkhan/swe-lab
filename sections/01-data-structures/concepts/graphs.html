Chapter 1 · Part 5

Dots and lines

P0 the start of every graph questionabout 50 minutes7 quick checks · 7 exercises

Friends on a social network, roads between cities, flights between airports, imports between modules, links between web pages: all of them are dots joined by lines. That shape is a graph, and a surprising number of interview questions are graph questions in disguise. Before you can search a graph (chapter 2), you have to store it, and how you store it decides what's cheap. This chapter is about that choice: three ways to write down the same dots and lines, what each one costs, and how to pick.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Describe any graph with the right words: directed or not, weighted or not, sparse or dense, and V and E.
  2. Store a graph as an edge list, an adjacency matrix and an adjacency list, and convert between them.
  3. Give the time of "are these two connected?" and "who are this one's neighbors?" and the memory, for each.
  4. Explain why walking a whole graph costs O(V + E) with lists and O(V²) with a matrix.
  5. Spot an implicit graph (a grid, a puzzle) and avoid building it at all.
  6. Pick the representation for a problem from its size and the questions it asks.

How to read this: go top to bottom. Answer each quick check before reading on; a wrong answer gets the idea explained a different way. Grey "Go deeper" boxes are optional on a first read. This chapter uses arrays and hash sets from Find anything in one step and trees from Sorted and fast.

1The problem: a billion people's friends

A social network has 1 billion users. The average user has 200 friends. The app asks two questions all day: "who are Ana's friends?" (to build her feed) and "are Ana and Ben friends?" (to decide if Ben can see her post).

Idea 1: a giant yes/no grid, one row and one column per user, with a tick where two people are friends. "Are Ana and Ben friends?" is one look at row Ana, column Ben. But the grid has 10⁹ × 10⁹ = 10¹⁸ cells. Even at one bit per cell that's 125 petabytes, and 99.99998% of it says "no".

Idea 2: one long list of every friendship, (Ana, Ben), (Ana, Cleo), … That's 100 billion pairs. Small enough to store, but "who are Ana's friends?" means reading all 100 billion.

The answer the real systems use: give each user their own list of friends, like the contacts in their phone. Ana's feed reads Ana's 200 entries and nothing else. All the lists together hold each friendship twice (once in each person's list), 200 billion ids, about 1.6 TB at 8 bytes each: large, but it can be split across machines. That's an adjacency list, and by the end of this chapter you'll know why it's the default, and when the grid or the long list wins instead.

2What a graph is

A graph is a set of things and a set of connections between pairs of them. The things are called vertices (or nodes); the connections are edges. We write V for the number of vertices and E for the number of edges. That's all. No positions, no distances, unless we add them.

   A ─── B           V = 5 vertices: A, B, C, D, E
   │   / │           E = 5 edges:    A–B, A–C, B–C, B–D, D–E
   │  /  │
   C     D ─── E     (where the dots sit on the page means nothing)

You've already met two special graphs. A linked list is a graph where each node has one edge to the next. A tree is a graph with no cycles (paths that come back to where they started), where there's exactly one path between any two nodes. A general graph drops both limits: any node can connect to any other, and following edges can bring you back where you started (a cycle).

Once you look for graphs, they're everywhere in interview problems:

  • Courses and prerequisites: "course B needs course A" is an edge A → B. "Can I finish every course?" means "is there a cycle?".
  • A maze or a map grid: each open cell is a vertex; neighboring open cells share an edge.
  • Word ladders: each word is a vertex; two words share an edge if they differ in one letter.
  • Network or service dependencies: "if this service goes down, who is affected?" is "who can reach it?".

A graph is vertices (things) plus edges (pairs of things that are connected). V counts vertices, E counts edges. Lists and trees are graphs with extra rules; a general graph can have any connections, including cycles.

3The kinds of graphs

Three questions describe almost any graph.

Does an edge have a direction?

Facebook friendship goes both ways: if Ana is Ben's friend, Ben is Ana's. That's an undirected edge. Following on Instagram doesn't: Ana can follow a celebrity who doesn't follow her back. That's a directed edge, an arrow from Ana to the celebrity. In a directed graph, each vertex has an out-degree (arrows leaving it: who you follow) and an in-degree (arrows arriving: your followers). In an undirected graph there's just the degree: how many edges touch it.

Does an edge carry a number?

A road map's edges have lengths; a flight network's have prices. That's a weighted graph: each edge has a weight. Unweighted graphs treat every edge as the same, like "1 hop".

How many edges, compared with how many there could be?

With V vertices, an undirected graph can have at most V(V − 1)/2 edges: every pair once, like the handshakes at a party. A directed graph can have V(V − 1): every ordered pair. A graph with close to that many edges is dense; one with far fewer is sparse.

Real graphs are almost always sparse. The social network could have 5 × 10¹⁷ friendships; it has 10¹¹, a fraction of about 0.00002%. A road network has about 2 to 3 roads per intersection, no matter how many intersections. Keep that number in your head; it decides the next three sections.

Go deeper: the rest of the vocabulary
  • Self-loop: an edge from a vertex to itself. Multigraph: two vertices can have more than one edge (two flights between the same cities). Most interview graphs have neither; ask.
  • Path: a sequence of edges joining one vertex to another. Connected: every vertex can reach every other (undirected). Connected component: a piece where everyone reaches everyone.
  • DAG: a directed acyclic graph, a directed graph with no cycles. Prerequisites, build systems and spreadsheets are DAGs, and they can be put in an order where every arrow points forward (a topological sort, chapter 2).
  • Bipartite: the vertices split into two groups with every edge going between the groups (users and the songs they liked).

GitHub lets you "star" a repository. Users star repos; repos don't star anything. Which kind of graph is "who starred what"?

Right. A star goes one way (user → repo), carries no number, and each user stars a tiny fraction of the hundreds of millions of repos. It's also bipartite: every edge goes from a user to a repo.

Ask the three questions in turn. Does it go both ways? No: you starring a repo doesn't make the repo star you, so it's directed. Is there a number on it? A star is just yes or no, so unweighted. How full is it? Each person stars maybe dozens of repos out of hundreds of millions, so nearly every possible edge is missing: sparse.

4Store it as a list of edges

The simplest way to write a graph down is how you'd describe it to a friend: a list of the pairs that are connected. That's an edge list.

edges = [("A", "B"), ("A", "C"), ("B", "C"), ("B", "D"), ("D", "E")]
roads = [("A", "B", 7), ("A", "C", 3)]     # weighted: (from, to, weight)

It takes O(E) memory, and it's how graphs usually arrive: a file of pairs, rows in a database table, the input of an interview problem. It's also exactly what some algorithms want. Kruskal's minimum spanning tree sorts all edges by weight and walks through them (chapter 2).

Its weakness is that it's in no useful order. "Who are B's neighbors?" means checking every edge to see if B is in it: O(E). "Is there an edge B–D?" is also O(E). For a walk around the graph, where you ask "neighbors of …?" once per vertex, that's O(V × E). So in most problems, the first thing you do with an edge list is turn it into something else.

A graph has 100,000 vertices and 1,000,000 edges, stored as an edge list. You ask "who are the neighbors of v?" once for every vertex. About how many edge reads is that?

Right. Each question scans all 10⁶ edges, and there are 10⁵ questions: 10¹¹ reads. With an adjacency list (section 6) the same job reads each edge about twice in total.

The edge list doesn't know which edges touch v, so every "neighbors of v?" has to read all 1,000,000 edges, even though v appears in only a few. Asking for each of the 100,000 vertices repeats that scan 100,000 times: 10⁵ × 10⁶ = 10¹¹. "Each edge once or twice" is what you get only after you've grouped the edges by vertex.

5Store it as a grid: the adjacency matrix

Picture the mileage chart printed in an old road atlas: every city down the side, every city across the top, and the distance in the box where a row meets a column. That's an adjacency matrix: a V × V grid where cell [i][j] says whether there's an edge from i to j (1 or 0, or the weight, with a blank or ∞ for "no road").

        A  B  C  D  E          (the graph from section 2, undirected)
   A  [ 0  1  1  0  0 ]
   B  [ 1  0  1  1  0 ]        row B: B touches A, C and D
   C  [ 1  1  0  0  0 ]
   D  [ 0  1  0  0  1 ]        undirected → the grid is a mirror image
   E  [ 0  0  0  1  0 ]        across the diagonal: [i][j] = [j][i]

Vertices need to be numbered 0 to V − 1 to be row and column indexes (keep a dict from name to number if they have names). Then:

  • "Is there an edge i → j?" One array lookup: O(1). This is the matrix's superpower.
  • "Who are i's neighbors?" Read the whole row: O(V), even if i has just one neighbor.
  • Memory: V² cells, always, however few edges there are.

For 1,000 vertices that's a million cells, which is nothing. For 1 million vertices it's 10¹², a terabyte even at one byte per cell. For the billion users of section 1, the 125 petabytes we already saw. The matrix pays for every edge that could exist, and in a sparse graph almost none do. (This is direct addressing from the hash map chapter, applied to pairs: perfect lookups, at the price of one slot per possible key.)

When is it right? When the graph is small or dense, or the algorithm itself is about all pairs. Floyd–Warshall (all-pairs shortest paths) fills in exactly this grid. And the matrix makes graph questions into arithmetic: multiply the matrix by itself and cell [i][j] counts the walks of length 2 from i to j.

An adjacency matrix is a V × V grid: O(1) to check one edge, O(V) to list a vertex's neighbors, and V² memory regardless of E. Use it for small or dense graphs; for big sparse ones it's almost all empty cells.

A road network has 10 million intersections and about 13 million road segments. Roughly how big is its adjacency matrix at 1 byte per cell?

Right: (10⁷)² = 10¹⁴ cells, 100 terabytes, to store 13 million roads. The same graph as adjacency lists is about 26 million entries (each road in two lists), about 200 MB.

The matrix doesn't care how many roads exist. It has a cell for every pair of intersections, road or not: 10 million rows × 10 million columns = 10¹⁴ cells. At a byte each that's 10¹⁴ bytes, 100 TB. Picture the mileage chart for 10 million towns: a page 10 million boxes wide and 10 million tall, nearly every box blank, because each town only has roads to two or three neighbors (13 million roads × 2 ends ÷ 10 million towns ≈ 2.6).

6Store each vertex's neighbors: the adjacency list

Back to the phone contacts. Nobody keeps a chart of the whole world's friendships; each person keeps a list of their own contacts. An adjacency list does the same: for each vertex, a list of the vertices it has an edge to.

adjacency list
adjacency matrix
One graph, two ways to store it. Pick a vertex: its neighbors light up in the drawing, its list and its matrix row. Count the cells each one reads to answer "who are its neighbors?". Tick "directed" and watch the matrix lose its mirror symmetry and each edge appear in only one list.

In Python, the usual way to build one from an edge list:

from collections import defaultdict

def build(edges, directed=False):
    adj = defaultdict(list)          # vertex -> list of neighbors
    for u, v in edges:
        adj[u].append(v)
        if not directed:
            adj[v].append(u)         # undirected: each edge goes in both lists
    return adj

# {"A": ["B", "C"], "B": ["A", "C", "D"], "C": ["A", "B"], "D": ["B", "E"], "E": ["D"]}

Now:

  • "Who are v's neighbors?" Read v's list: O(degree of v). Ana's 200 friends, not a billion cells.
  • "Is there an edge u → v?" Scan u's list: O(degree of u). If you need this a lot, store a set instead of a list: O(1) on average, like the matrix, at some memory cost.
  • Memory: O(V + E): one list per vertex, one entry per edge (two for undirected).

The handshake fact: why a full walk is O(V + E)

Here's the fact that makes adjacency lists the default. Add up the lengths of every list. Each undirected edge u–v appears exactly twice: once in u's list and once in v's. So the total of all the degrees is exactly 2E. (At a party, if you add up how many hands each person shook, you count every handshake twice, once per hand.) For a directed graph, each edge is in one list, so the total is E.

Now picture any algorithm that visits every vertex once and, at each one, looks through its neighbors. With adjacency lists the total work is V (visiting) + 2E (all the lists, added up): O(V + E). With a matrix, each "look through the neighbors" reads a whole row of V cells, so it's V × V: O(V²), however sparse the graph. For the road network above, that's about 36 million steps (10 million + 2 × 13 million) versus 10¹⁴.

An adjacency list stores, for each vertex, its neighbors: O(degree) to list them, O(V + E) memory. Because all the lists together hold 2E entries (E if directed), visiting every vertex and every edge costs O(V + E). That's why it's the default for sparse graphs.

An undirected graph has 6 vertices with degrees 3, 2, 2, 1, 1 and 1. How many edges does it have?

Right. The degrees add up to 10, and each edge is counted twice (once at each end), so E = 10 / 2 = 5.

Picture each edge as a handshake. A person's degree is how many hands they shook. Adding everyone's count gives 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 10, but every handshake involves two people, so it was counted twice. 10 counts of hands means 5 handshakes. (The number of edges has nothing to do with the number of vertices: 6 people could shake 0 hands or 15.)

Memory for a graph with V vertices and the chosen average degree: a matrix at 1 bit per cell versus adjacency lists at 8 bytes per entry (each undirected edge stored twice) plus 8 bytes per vertex for its list. Raise V and watch the matrix explode while the lists grow in step with the edges.

A rule of thumb: default to adjacency lists. Switch to a matrix when V is at most a few thousand and either the graph is dense or you need lots of O(1) edge checks. Keep the edge list when the algorithm wants edges sorted or processed one at a time.

Three follow-ups interviewers like here:

  • Both directions. A directed adjacency list answers "who does v follow?" in O(degree). "Who follows v?" would mean scanning every list. If you need both, store both: out-lists and in-lists (the transpose, exercise 4). Twice the memory, and every new edge is written twice.
  • Averages hide supernodes. "200 friends on average" is true, but follower counts follow a power law: most people have a few hundred, a celebrity has 100 million. "List my followers" is O(degree), so for that one vertex it's 10⁸, and the machine holding that list becomes a hot spot. Real systems shard by user (each user's lists live on the machine that owns that user) and give supernodes special treatment, such as splitting their lists across machines or precomputing results.
  • Python's real cost. The 8 bytes per entry is a pointer. In CPython each list also has a header of about 56 bytes, and an int above 256 is its own 28-byte object unless it's shared, so a list of lists costs several times the estimate. Exercise 6 measures it.

In Python

  • Vertices are 0 to n − 1: adj = [[] for _ in range(n)]. Fast and compact.
  • Vertices have names: defaultdict(list), as above.
  • Weighted: store pairs, adj[u].append((v, w)), or a dict of dicts, adj[u][v] = w, which also gives O(1) edge checks.
  • Vertices with no edges at all won't appear in a defaultdict unless you add them. If the problem says "n vertices", create all n.

You have 2,000 airports and want, for every pair, whether some airline flies directly between them; you'll check pairs millions of times. Which representation?

Right. 2,000² = 4 million cells is tiny (4 MB as bytes), and every check is one O(1) lookup. The default doesn't apply when V is small and the main question is "is there an edge?".

Run the numbers before reaching for the default. The matrix would have 2,000 × 2,000 = 4 million cells: about 4 MB, nothing at all. In exchange, every one of the millions of "is there a direct flight?" checks is a single lookup. An adjacency list makes each check scan an airport's routes, and an edge list scans every flight. A list of sets would also give O(1) checks; the matrix is simpler and smaller here.

8Graphs you never build: implicit graphs

A maze on a 1,000 × 1,000 grid is a graph: each open cell is a vertex, and each pair of open cells side by side shares an edge. You could build adjacency lists for all million cells first. But you don't have to: the neighbors of cell (r, c) are just (r − 1, c), (r + 1, c), (r, c − 1) and (r, c + 1), whichever are inside the grid and open. You can compute them the moment you need them.

def neighbors(grid, r, c):
    for dr, dc in ((-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)):
        nr, nc = r + dr, c + dc
        if 0 <= nr < len(grid) and 0 <= nc < len(grid[0]) and grid[nr][nc] != "#":
            yield nr, nc

That's an implicit graph: the edges exist as a rule, not as stored data. It saves all the memory of the lists and the time to build them. Many interview "graphs" are like this:

  • Grids: islands, mazes, flood fill, rotting oranges.
  • Word ladders: the neighbors of "cold" are the dictionary words one letter away. (Tip: generate patterns like "c*ld" instead of comparing every pair of words.)
  • Puzzle states: the vertices of a sliding puzzle or a lock with 4 dials are its states; the edges are single moves. The lock has 10⁴ = 10,000 states, each with 8 neighbors (each dial one step up or down). You'd never write those down.

Sometimes there are too many vertices to even count: the positions of the board game Go form a graph with about 2 × 10¹⁷⁰ vertices, far more than the 10⁸⁰ atoms in the universe. Search only ever touches the part it explores, which is the only reason such problems can be solved at all.

If you can compute a vertex's neighbors from the vertex itself (a grid cell, a word, a puzzle state), don't build the graph. Generate neighbors on demand: no memory for edges, no build time, and it works even when the graph is too big to write down.

A grid of 1,000 × 1,000 open cells, each connected to its up, down, left and right neighbors. About how many edges does the graph have?

Right. Most cells have degree 4, and the degrees add up to about 4 million, but each edge is shared by two cells, so E ≈ 2 million (exactly 2 × 1,000 × 999 = 1,998,000). Sparse: degree 4 out of a possible million.

Use the handshake fact from section 6. Each cell has up to 4 neighbors, so the degrees add up to about 4 million. But the edge between a cell and its right-hand neighbor is the same edge as the one between that neighbor and its left-hand neighbor: it's counted from both ends. So E is about 4 million / 2 = 2 million. Another way: each cell "owns" the edge to its right and the edge below it, 2 per cell.

9A first walk: why you need "visited"

Chapter 2 covers searching graphs properly (breadth-first and depth-first search). Here's the one idea that comes with the data structure itself. In a tree, walking down from the root never revisits a node. In a graph, edges can lead back to where you've been, and a walk that doesn't notice will go round a cycle forever.

So every graph walk keeps a visited set (or an array of booleans, for vertices 0 to n − 1). Before stepping to a neighbor, check it; if it's new, mark it at once, as you push it onto the to-do list. (Marking only when you later take it off the list lets the same vertex be pushed once per arrow into it, so the list can grow to O(E).) Each vertex is then entered once, and each of its edges is looked at once from that end: O(V + E) with adjacency lists, exactly the handshake count.

Find everything reachable from 0 in a directed graph that contains a cycle (0 → 1 → 2 → 0). The walk keeps a to-do stack. Watch the total of arrow looks. Then tick "forget the visited set" and watch it go round the cycle with no end.
Go deeper: how real systems store huge graphs
  • Compressed sparse row (CSR): instead of V separate lists, two flat arrays. targets holds every list back to back; offsets[v] says where v's neighbors start, so they are targets[offsets[v] : offsets[v + 1]]. No per-list overhead and perfectly cache-friendly. Graph libraries, sparse-matrix code and graph neural network frameworks use it. The catch: adding an edge means rebuilding. Exercise 4 builds one.
  • Social graphs: Facebook's TAO stores objects and their edge lists in sharded MySQL with a big cache in front; queries are "the edges of type T leaving object X", an adjacency list lookup.
  • Road networks: sparse (2 to 3 edges per intersection) and nearly planar, which routing engines exploit with precomputed shortcuts (contraction hierarchies).
  • Graph databases (Neo4j) store each node with direct pointers to its edges, so a hop costs the same no matter how big the graph is ("index-free adjacency").

You walk a directed graph with a matrix, visiting each vertex once and scanning its row for neighbors. The graph has 10,000 vertices and 20,000 edges. Roughly how many cells do you read?

Right. Each of 10⁴ vertices scans a row of 10⁴ cells: 10⁸, even though only 20,000 cells contain a 1. Adjacency lists would do about 30,000 steps.

V + E is the cost with adjacency lists, where each vertex's list holds only its real neighbors. A matrix row has a cell for every possible neighbor, all 10,000 of them, and you have to read every cell to find the few 1s. Once per vertex: 10,000 × 10,000 = 100 million reads. The matrix makes a walk O(V²) whatever E is.

10What to say in the interview

When a problem turns out to be a graph, say how you'll store it before you search it:

"This is a graph: courses are vertices and each prerequisite is a directed edge. Up to 10⁵ courses and 10⁵ prerequisites, so it's sparse, and a matrix would be 10¹⁰ cells. I'll build an adjacency list from the edge list in O(V + E), with a list for all n courses, including ones with no edges. Then any traversal is O(V + E) with a visited array. If they also wanted fast 'is A a direct prerequisite of B?' checks, I'd use sets for the neighbor lists."

Habits that make you sound senior:

  • Name V and E, and say whether the graph is directed, weighted, and sparse or dense.
  • Give costs in V and E: O(V + E), not "O(n)".
  • Justify the representation from the sizes and from the questions asked.
  • Spot implicit graphs and don't build them.
  • Mention vertices with no edges, self-loops and duplicate edges, and how your code treats them.

✓What to remember

  1. A graph is vertices plus edges: directed or not, weighted or not. V and E are its sizes.
  2. Max edges: V(V − 1)/2 undirected, V(V − 1) directed. Real graphs are sparse.
  3. Edge list: O(E) memory, but "neighbors of v" is O(E). Usually a starting point to convert.
  4. Matrix: O(1) edge check, O(V) neighbors, O(V²) memory. Small or dense graphs only.
  5. Adjacency list: O(deg) neighbors, O(V + E) memory. The default. Sets for O(1) edge checks.
  6. The degrees add up to 2E, so a full walk is O(V + E) with lists and O(V²) with a matrix.
  7. Implicit graphs (grids, words, states): generate neighbors on demand, never build.
  8. Every walk needs a visited set, or cycles make it loop forever.

Warm-up · pen and paper

1. Three ways to write one graph

A directed graph on vertices 0–4 has edges 0→1, 0→2, 1→2, 2→0, 3→4. Write its adjacency list and adjacency matrix. Give every vertex's in-degree and out-degree, and check that each column adds up to the in-degree.

Answer

List: 0: [1, 2], 1: [2], 2: [0], 3: [4], 4: []. Matrix rows: 0: [0 1 1 0 0], 1: [0 0 1 0 0], 2: [1 0 0 0 0], 3: [0 0 0 0 1], 4: [0 0 0 0 0]. Out-degrees (row sums): 2, 1, 1, 1, 0. In-degrees (column sums): 1, 1, 2, 0, 1. Both add up to 5 = E, as they must: every arrow leaves one vertex and arrives at one. Vertex 4 has an empty list; in a defaultdict it would be missing unless you add it.

2. Size it

For each graph, estimate the adjacency matrix (1 bit per cell) and the adjacency list (8 bytes per entry), and pick one: (a) 500 people at a conference, everyone has met about half of the others; (b) 50 million web pages with 20 links each; (c) a 2,000 × 2,000 grid maze.

Answer

(a) Matrix: 500² = 250,000 bits ≈ 31 KB. List: 500 × 250 = 125,000 entries ≈ 1 MB. Dense and tiny: matrix. (b) Matrix: (5 × 10⁷)² = 2.5 × 10¹⁵ bits ≈ 312 TB. List: 10⁹ links (directed, stored once) ≈ 8 GB. List, and on one machine you'd want CSR or compression. (c) Matrix: (4 × 10⁶)² = 1.6 × 10¹³ bits = 2 TB. List: 4 million cells × 4 neighbors ≈ 16 million entries ≈ 128 MB. Neither: it's implicit, compute neighbors from (r, c), 0 bytes.

Warm-up · pen and paper

3. Spot the graph

Say what the vertices and edges are, and whether it's directed or weighted: (a) "Given flights with prices, find the cheapest route with at most k stops." (b) "Given a list of accounts with emails, merge accounts that share an email." (c) "Given a beginWord, endWord and a dictionary, find the shortest transformation sequence."

Answer

(a) Airports are vertices; flights are directed, weighted edges (price). (b) Accounts (or emails) are vertices; an undirected edge joins two accounts that share an email, and each merged account is a connected component (union-find, topic 01.07, also fits). (c) Words are vertices; an undirected, unweighted edge joins words one letter apart. Implicit: generate the neighbors, don't compare every pair.
