Chapter 1 · Part 7

Same group or not?

P1 union-find (disjoint sets) with path compressionabout 50 minutes10 quick checks · 8 exercises

Some questions are about groups that only ever grow. Which accounts belong to the same person? Which computers can already talk to each other? Which pixels form one blob? Which towns does the road network already connect? Two requests arrive millions of times, mixed together: "these two belong together" and "are these two together?". Union-find answers both in a few steps each on average, and for any input you will ever meet, that average doesn't grow. It's about 15 lines of code, and every line has a reason. This chapter builds it from a list of labels, one fix at a time.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Say what union-find answers, and what it can't (splitting a group, finding a path, one-way links).
  2. Explain why a label on every item makes merging cost O(n), and how parent pointers fix that.
  3. Prove that union by size keeps every item within log₂ n steps of its root.
  4. Explain what path compression does to the tree, and why the first find pays for the ones after it.
  5. Say what "amortized O(α(n))" means, and why a single find can still take about log₂ n steps.
  6. Use it to count groups, catch cycles, link accounts, track growing islands and build a minimum spanning tree with Kruskal's algorithm.

1The problem: is this payment part of a fraud ring?

You work on fraud at a payments company with 10 million accounts. Fraudsters run many accounts at once, and they slip: two accounts use the same card, the same phone number, the same laptop. Each slip arrives as a link: "account 4,182 and account 977,301 share a device". About 30 million links arrive a day.

Before approving a payment, the system asks: "is the account paying right now in the same ring as an account we've already banned?" That's 100 million questions a day, and each answer is needed in milliseconds.

A ring is a connected component: accounts joined by a chain of links, even when two of them never shared anything directly. You know how to find one: run a BFS from the account. But one search can walk every account and every link, 10 million + 2 × 30 million = 70 million steps. Do that for 100 million questions and it's 7 × 10¹⁵ steps: at a billion steps a second, about 81 days of computing for one day of payments.

The structure in this chapter takes in each new link, and answers each question, with a handful of memory reads. The day's 130 million operations become under a billion memory reads: at most a couple of minutes on one machine, even if every read went all the way to main memory (about 100 nanoseconds each). It's called union-find, also disjoint-set union (DSU). Beyond fraud rings, it's what Kruskal's minimum spanning tree algorithm runs on, how image libraries group pixels into blobs, and the classic interview answer for "accounts merge", "number of provinces" and "redundant connection".

A teammate says: "Run BFS once over all the links, write each account's ring number into an array, then answer each question by comparing two ring numbers." What goes wrong?

Right. The labels are perfect for one frozen moment. The 30 million links a day keep merging rings, and every merge makes some labels wrong. Rerunning the BFS after every link is 30 million × 70 million steps. We need labels that can be merged cheaply, and that's the whole chapter.

Put numbers on it. 10 million ring numbers at 8 bytes each is 80 MB: fine. Comparing two numbers is one step: as fast as anything can be. The trouble is time, not space: at 9:00 accounts 5 and 9 are in different rings; at 9:01 a link arrives that joins the ring of 5 with the ring of 9. Every account in one of those rings now has the wrong number. Fixing it with a fresh BFS after each of 30 million links costs 30 million × 70 million steps, about 24 days per day.

2Groups that only merge

Strip the fraud story down to what the computer must do. There are n items, numbered 0 to n − 1. Picture a party where everyone starts out standing alone. Two kinds of requests arrive, in any order:

  • union(a, b): "a and b are together". The circle around a and the circle around b join into one circle.
  • connected(a, b): "are a and b in the same circle right now?"

The power is in one fact you already trust: if a is with b, and b is with c, then a is with c. Circles join whole, so a link between two people pulls their entire circles together:

 start           {a} {b} {c} {d} {e}
 union(a, b)     {a, b} {c} {d} {e}
 union(c, d)     {a, b} {c, d} {e}
 union(b, c)     {a, b, c, d} {e}    ← a and d are together now,
                                       though no link joins them
 connected(a, d) → yes     connected(a, e) → no

Every item is in exactly one group at all times: groups never overlap and nobody is left out. Groups like that are called disjoint sets (disjoint means "sharing nothing"). To answer "same group?", we give each group a name. The trick is to name a group after one of its own members, its representative, like a team captain. Then the main operation is find(x): "who is the captain of x's group?", and connected(a, b) is just find(a) == find(b). Which member is captain doesn't matter, as long as everyone in the group agrees.

Where the party picture breaks: real circles at a party split up again. These groups never split, and union-find has no "un-union". Section 10 covers what to do when you need one.

Union-find keeps items in disjoint groups that only ever merge. union(a, b) joins a's whole group with b's whole group; find(x) names x's group by one of its members (the representative); connected(a, b) is find(a) == find(b).

Six items, 0 to 5, all starting alone. You call union(0, 1), union(2, 3), union(1, 3) and union(4, 5). Is connected(0, 2) true?

Right. After union(0, 1) and union(2, 3) there are groups {0, 1} and {2, 3}. union(1, 3) joins those whole groups, so {0, 1, 2, 3} is one group. Groups join whole, so "together" travels along any chain of links.

Draw the circles after each call. union(0, 1): {0, 1}. union(2, 3): {2, 3}. union(1, 3): 1's circle and 3's circle merge, the whole circles, not just the two people, so now {0, 1, 2, 3}. union(4, 5): {4, 5}. 0 and 2 are in the same circle. The order doesn't matter either: after the same set of unions, in any order, the circles are the same, because each union only ever merges.

3First try: a label on everyone

The simplest design is the teammate's idea from section 1, made mergeable. Give every item a team shirt: a label, the number of its group. Two items are together exactly when their shirts match.

label = list(range(n))          # everyone starts in their own team

def connected(a, b):
    return label[a] == label[b]  # one comparison

def union(a, b):
    la, lb = label[a], label[b]
    for x in range(n):           # repaint team lb as team la,
        if label[x] == lb:       # checking every shirt there is
            label[x] = la

connected is O(1). union is the problem: the answer "which group is x in?" is written down on every single member, so changing the answer means touching every member. Scanning all the labels is O(n) per union. For the fraud system that's 10 million labels for each of 30 million links: 3 × 10¹⁴ steps, about 3.5 days of computing for each day of links.

This design is called quick-find: find is instant, union pays for it. The flaw has a precise shape, and the next section attacks exactly it: the group's name is copied into every member.

Go deeper: repaint only the smaller team

Keep a list of members for each team, and when two teams merge, repaint the smaller one and append its list to the bigger one. A single merge can still repaint 5 million shirts. But follow one item: every time it is repainted, its team at least doubles (it was in the smaller team, and the two are now one). Starting from a team of 1, it can double at most log₂ n times before the team holds everyone. So all the merges together repaint at most n log₂ n shirts: for 10 million accounts, at most about 23 repaints each, 2.3 × 10⁸ in total. That's a perfectly good structure, and the trick is common enough to have a name, small-to-large merging. Keep this doubling argument in mind: section 5 uses it again. Union-find beats it anyway: no member lists to store, and about one step per operation instead of up to log₂ n.

A label per item (quick-find) makes connected O(1) but union O(n), because every member carries a copy of its group's name. Any design where a merge must update every member will be slow.

With one label per item and a union that scans the label list, what does the 1,000th union cost when n = 1,000,000?

Right. The scan doesn't know where team lb's members are, so it checks all n shirts, every time, even if the team has two members. Member lists can cut that to the team's size, but as soon as two big teams merge, that's millions of writes too.

Look at the loop: for x in range(n). It visits every item there is, because nothing records where team lb's members are. So every union costs n = 1,000,000 checks, no matter how small the teams are. And a union really does have to change every member's label, not one: if only one shirt were repainted, the other members of team lb would still claim the old team.

4Point, don't repaint

Here's the fix. Instead of a shirt with the group's name, each person holds a note naming one other person in their group: "ask her". Follow the notes and you end at someone whose note names themselves. That person is the group's representative, called the root. To find your group, keep following notes until someone points at themselves.

Now look at what a merge costs. To join two groups, find both roots, and change one note: one root now points at the other root. Everyone in the first group still reaches the second root, by following their notes one step further than before. Nobody else's note changes.

Arrows that only ever lead up to a root make a tree (each group is one tree, with the root at the top), and all the groups together are a forest. It fits in one list: parent[x] is the person x's note names, and parent[x] == x means x is a root.

parent = list(range(n))        # everyone starts as a root

def find(x):
    while parent[x] != x:      # follow the notes upward...
        x = parent[x]
    return x                   # ...to whoever points at itself

def union(a, b):
    ra, rb = find(a), find(b)
    if ra == rb:
        return False           # already one group
    parent[ra] = rb            # ONE write merges two groups
    return True
Eight items, the code above. Arrows point from a child to its parent. Green: a root (it points at itself). Yellow: the items find is walking through (their arrows are highlighted too). Blue item: the one whose parent pointer a union just wrote. The list underneath is parent; the tree is just a drawing of it. Step through and count the writes per union.

A union is now two finds plus one write. And a find costs one step per level it climbs, so everything depends on how tall the trees get. Here's the bad news. Call union(0, 1), then union(0, 2), union(0, 3), and so on. Each time, the root of 0's tree goes under a lone newcomer, so the tree grows into a line, and each find(0) climbs the whole line:

 union(0, 1)       0 → 1
 union(0, 2)       0 → 1 → 2           find(0) climbed 1 step
 union(0, 3)       0 → 1 → 2 → 3       find(0) climbed 2 steps
 …
 union(0, n − 1)   0 → 1 → … → n − 1   find(0) climbed n − 2

 total: 1 + 2 + … + (n − 2) ≈ n²/2 steps
 n = 10 million: 5 × 10¹³ steps, about 14 hours of work,
 just to merge the accounts

This is called quick-union. We traded "every union touches every member" for "every find climbs the whole tree". It's the same problem as a plain binary search tree fed sorted keys: nothing controls the shape. The next two sections fix the shape, one from each end: keep trees short when they're built (section 5), and flatten them when they're used (section 6).

Store a parent pointer per item instead of a label: each group is a tree, its root is the representative, and find climbs to the root. Union becomes one pointer write after two finds. But with no rule for which root goes under which, trees can grow into lines, and find becomes O(n).

parent = [1, 3, 3, 3, 5, 3, 5, 7]. What does find(6) return, and how many steps does it climb?

Right: 6 → parent[6] = 5 → parent[5] = 3, and parent[3] = 3, so 3 is the root. Two groups exist here: the roots are the items that point at themselves, 3 and 7.

find keeps going until it reaches an item that points at itself. Start at 6: parent[6] = 5, and 5 isn't a root, because parent[5] = 3, not 5. Go on to 3: parent[3] = 3, so stop. The answer is 3, after two steps. 5 is only a middle manager. And "nothing points at 6" makes 6 a leaf (the bottom of a tree), not a root: a root is what something points up to, and what points at itself.

5Fix 1: hang the smaller tree under the bigger one

Look at why the line formed. Every union hung the big tree under a lone item, so every member of the big tree got one step further from its root, every time. The fix is a choice we were making blindly: which root goes under which.

Picture two companies merging: a 5-person startup and a 5,000-person firm. If the startup's boss reports to the big firm's boss, 5 people get one more level of management above them. Do it the other way round and 5,000 people do. So: always hang the smaller tree's root under the bigger tree's root. Keep size[r], the number of items in the tree with root r. In union, compare the two roots' sizes, point the smaller root at the bigger one, and add the sizes. (On a tie, either way is fine; add the sizes the same way.) That's union by size.

Why no item ends up more than log₂ n steps from its root

This is the argument interviewers want, and it's short. Follow one item, x. Its distance to the root (its depth) only grows when its root is hung under another root, and then it grows by exactly 1. Union by size only does that when x's tree is the smaller one (or a tie). So the merged tree is at least twice as big as x's old tree. Every step deeper, x's group at least doubles:

 x's depth     x's group has at least
     0              1 item
     1              2 items
     2              4 items
     3              8 items
     …              …
     d              2^d items,  and that can't be more than n

 so 2^d ≤ n, which means d ≤ log₂ n
 n = 1,000:  depth ≤ 9        n = 10 million:  depth ≤ 23
 n = 1 billion:  depth ≤ 29

And the bound is reached: merge 16 items in equal pairs (8 trees of 2, then 4 of 4, then 2 of 8, then 1 of 16), and each round puts the deepest items one level lower. After 4 rounds the deepest item is at depth 4 = log₂ 16. No order of unions can do worse.

Explain it another way: you only get a new boss when your company doubles

Think of depth as the number of bosses above you. Under union by size you only get a new boss when your company merges into one at least as big as yours, so your company at least doubles each time. Start in a company of 1. After 10 new bosses, your company has at least 2¹⁰ = 1,024 people; after 23, at least 2²³ = 8,388,608. A 10-million-person world can't fit a 24th doubling (that needs 16.7 million), so nobody ever has more than 23 bosses above them. Where the picture breaks: real companies hire one person at a time. Here groups only grow by merging, and the doubling is what limits the depth.

With that, find is O(log n) in the worst case, and so is union (two finds plus O(1) work). For the fraud system that's at most 23 steps per find, instead of up to 10 million.

Go deeper: union by rank, and why not track the real height

Many textbooks use union by rank instead. Each root keeps a rank, an upper bound on its tree's height. A lone item has rank 0. In union, the root with the lower rank goes under the root with the higher rank and no rank changes; on a tie, either goes under the other and the winner's rank goes up by 1. A root of rank r always has at least 2ʳ items (two rank-(r − 1) trees were needed to make it), so rank, and height, stay ≤ log₂ n. Same guarantee as size.

Why "upper bound on height" and not the height itself? Because of section 6: path compression shortens trees, and keeping exact heights correct would mean re-measuring a tree after every find. That costs more than it saves. A rank is allowed to go stale (it's never too small), and the proofs only need that. Size has one practical bonus: it answers "how big is x's group?" for free. Either one is a fine answer in an interview.

Union by size: point the smaller tree's root at the bigger tree's root. An item only gets one step deeper when its group at least doubles, so no item is ever more than log₂ n steps from its root. find and union become O(log n) in the worst case.

With union by size, 1,000,000 items and any order of unions you like, how far can an item end up from its root?

Right. Each extra step down means the item's group at least doubled, and 2²⁰ = 1,048,576 is already more than a million, so an item can go at most 19 levels down: log₂ 1,000,000 ≈ 19.9, rounded down.

The order can't hurt any more: the size rule decides which root goes under which, whatever order the unions arrive in. And hanging a tree under a root doesn't flatten that tree: items already 3 deep in the smaller tree become 4 deep. What limits depth is doubling. An item gets one step deeper only when its group at least doubles: 1, 2, 4, 8, … After 19 steps its group has at least 524,288 items; a 20th step would need 1,048,576, more than exist. So 19 is the most.

6Fix 2: path compression

Union by size caps a climb at 23 steps for 10 million accounts. But look at what the fraud system actually does: the same busy account pays again and again, and every time, find climbs the same 23 notes to the same root. The answer hasn't changed. We're paying for the same walk over and over.

Picture climbing a chain of managers to find the CEO. Once you know who the CEO is, walk back down and tell every manager you passed: "you can report straight to the CEO now". You did the climb once; nobody on that path ever has to do it again. That's path compression: after find(x) reaches the root, point every item on the path it walked directly at the root.

A tall tree (built without union by size, so the effect is big). find(0) climbs 4 steps, then re-points everyone it passed straight at the root, 4. Watch the later finds: 6 and 5 got shorter too, because their paths ran through 1 and 2. The step counter shows how many steps each find climbed.

Three things to notice, because each one is a follow-up question:

  1. Nobody changes group. Every item still leads to the same root; only the route got shorter. Items that weren't on the path keep their pointers, and their routes get shorter too if they ran through the path.
  2. Sizes never need fixing. Sizes (or ranks) are only kept at roots, and compression never changes which items are roots or how many items each tree has.
  3. The first find pays extra, the rest ride free. The walk that compresses a path costs about twice a plain walk (up once, then re-point once), and every later find through that path costs about one step. Paying once up front and spreading it over later operations is exactly amortized cost, from the complexity chapter.

Before and after, in the list itself:

 before: [1, 2, 3, 4, 4]   find(0): 0 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4, 4 steps
 after:  [4, 4, 4, 4, 4]   find(0), find(1), find(2): 1 step each

There are a few ways to write it. Two passes: climb once to find the root, then climb the same path again setting each parent to the root. Recursion: find(parent[x]) returns the root, and on the way back out each item stores it. Path halving: while climbing, point each item at its grandparent (two steps up) and continue from there. One pass and no second walk; the path only halves instead of flattening, but repeated finds flatten it, and the overall guarantee is the same (Tarjan and van Leeuwen proved that in 1984). Any of them is fine in an interview; say which one you chose.

Go deeper: recursion depth in Python

The recursive version is the shortest to write, and it's fine with union by size: trees are at most about 30 deep. Without union by size, a chain of 10,000 items (section 4's bad order makes one easily) makes the first recursive find go 10,000 calls deep, and Python stops at its default limit of 1,000 with a RecursionError. The two-pass loop and path halving use no recursion at all. One more reason to use both fixes, not just one.

Path compression: after find walks from x to the root, point every item on that path straight at the root. Nobody changes group, sizes don't need updating, and later finds through that path take about one step. The first find pays for the shortcut.

parent = [1, 2, 3, 3, 1], with full path compression. You call find(4). What is parent afterwards?

Right. find(4) climbs 4 → 1 → 2 → 3, so 4, 1 and 2 are on the path; each now points at 3 (2 already did). Item 0 wasn't on the path, so it keeps pointing at 1. It's still in the group, and still 2 steps from the root, until some find walks through it.

Compression only touches the path the find actually walked. Trace it: 4 → parent[4] = 1 → parent[1] = 2 → parent[2] = 3, and 3 is the root. Everyone on that walk (4, 1 and 2) now points at 3: that's more than just 4. Item 0 wasn't on it (nothing led from 4 through 0), so parent[0] stays 1. Compression is lazy on purpose: it fixes the routes it uses, and leaves the rest for later finds.

7How fast is it, really?

We have two fixes, one that keeps trees short as they're built and one that flattens them as they're used. Does either one matter once you have the other? Try them on the same work: n − 1 unions in some order, then n finds of random items.

n =
The same unions and finds, run four ways. "Steps" counts every move up a parent pointer during finds (re-pointing for compression costs about as much again on the walks that do it). Slide n up on each input. Worst order: the plain version grows with n, and either fix alone stops it. Equal-sized merges: union by size alone builds trees exactly log₂ n tall, and its steps creep up with n. Random pairs: even random unions make the plain trees tall. With both fixes, steps per operation stay near 1 on every input.

Here's what is proven about each combination (m operations on n items):

VersionWorst single findm operations, total
Plain quick-unionO(n)O(m · n)
Union by size (or rank) onlyO(log n)O(m log n)
Path compression onlyO(n)about O(m log n)
BothO(log n)O(m · α(n))

That last line is Robert Tarjan's 1975 result, and α needs a picture. Start with a function that grows ridiculously slowly. log* n ("log star") counts how many times you press the log₂ button on a calculator before the number drops to 1 or less:

 16          16 → 4 → 2 → 1                       log* = 3
 65,536      65,536 → 16 → 4 → 2 → 1              log* = 4
 2^65,536    2^65,536 → 65,536 → 16 → 4 → 2 → 1   log* = 5

 2^65,536 has 19,729 digits. The observable universe has
 about 10^80 atoms: an 81-digit number.

Hopcroft and Ullman proved in 1973 that both fixes together cost O(log* n) per operation, on average over any sequence. So, at most 5 for any input that could exist. Tarjan then tightened it to α(n), the inverse Ackermann function, which grows even more slowly than log* n. The Ackermann function is a function that explodes faster than towers of powers; its inverse is correspondingly flat. α(n) is at most 4 for every n up to a number far larger than 10⁸⁰. For anything you'll ever run, α(n) ≤ 4.

"Amortized" is doing real work in that sentence

The guarantee is about the total: any sequence of m operations costs O(m · α(n)), so the average per operation is O(α(n)). A single find can still be slow. Right after the equal-sized merges in the figure, a find on a deepest item climbs about log₂ n steps (12 when n = 8,192, where log₂ n = 13), because no find has walked that path yet; the unions' own finds flattened only a few paths. What compression guarantees is that this is paid once: the climb flattens the path, and the expensive walks can't keep happening. Union by size is what bounds the single worst find at O(log n); compression is what makes the average flat.

So in an interview: "O(α(n)) amortized per operation, effectively constant". Don't call it O(1): it provably isn't. Fredman and Saks showed in 1989 that every union-find structure needs about α(n) steps per operation on average in the worst case. Tarjan's version is as fast as this problem allows.

Go deeper: where log* comes from (a sketch)

Use union by rank and full compression. Three facts: ranks strictly increase going up any path; a node's rank never changes once it stops being a root; and at most n / 2ʳ nodes ever have rank r. Now sort ranks into blocks whose ends are towers of 2: {0, 1}, {2}, {3, 4}, {5 … 16}, {17 … 65,536}, and so on. There are only log* n blocks.

Charge each step of a find to one of two accounts. If the step crosses into a higher block, charge the find: at most log* n such steps per find. Otherwise charge the node being stepped from. Compression then gives that node a new parent (the root) of strictly higher rank, so a node can be charged at most as many times as there are ranks in its block before its parent lands in a higher block, after which it is never charged again. Few nodes have high ranks, and the block sizes and the n / 2ʳ counts cancel: each block's nodes are charged at most n times in total. That's O(n log* n) for the nodes plus O(m log* n) for the finds. Tarjan's α bound uses the same idea with a much finer set of blocks.

With union by size and path compression, any m operations cost O(m · α(n)) in total, where α(n) ≤ 4 for any real n: effectively constant per operation, amortized. A single find can still take up to log₂ n steps; union by size bounds the worst one, compression makes the average flat.

With both fixes and n = 1,000,000, can a single find ever climb more than 5 steps?

Right. Union by size keeps every item within 19 steps of its root (section 5), and a path nobody has walked yet can be that long. The α bound is amortized: that long walk flattens its path, so long walks can't keep happening, and the total over many operations stays about α(n) each.

Two different promises are mixed up here. Union by size bounds the worst single find: no item is more than log₂ n ≈ 19 steps from its root, whatever the order (so not n − 1). Compression bounds the average: the first walk up a long path flattens it, so the next walks are short, and over m operations the total is O(m · α(n)). Right after a batch of unions, before any find has walked anything, a deep item really is up to 19 steps away. Like a gym membership: one big payment, then cheap visits. The average is low; the first day isn't.

8Using it: count, catch cycles, link, grow

Most union-find interview problems are one of four shapes. In each one the work is spotting what the items are and when to call union; the structure itself doesn't change.

Counting groups

Keep a counter that starts at n (everyone alone). Every union that returns True merged two groups into one, so subtract 1. Now "how many groups?" is O(1) at any moment, even while links keep arriving: "number of provinces", "how many friend circles after each new friendship", "when did everyone become connected?" (the first moment the counter hits 1).

Catching a cycle

Go through the edges of an undirected graph, calling union on each edge's two ends. If union returns False, the two ends were already connected by earlier edges, so this edge closes a loop. That's "redundant connection": find the edge that turns a tree into something with a cycle.

def first_cycle_edge(n, edges):
    uf = UnionFind(n)
    for u, v in edges:
        if not uf.union(u, v):   # u and v were already connected...
            return (u, v)        # ...so this edge closes a loop
    return None                  # no loop: the edges form a forest

This only works for undirected graphs. Union-find has no notion of direction: an edge a → b merges a's group and b's group, as if it went both ways. For "can a reach b?" along one-way edges, or a cycle in a directed graph, use DFS with three colors (topic 02.05).

Items that aren't numbers

Union-find wants items 0 to n − 1. Real items are emails, phone numbers, device ids. Give each one a number the first time you see it, with a dict. In the fraud problem: each account has a few identifiers. Keep a dict from identifier to the first account that had it. When another account has the same identifier, union the two accounts. One owner per identifier is enough: everyone who shares it ends up unioned with that owner, so they're all in one group. That's O(total identifiers), never comparing accounts pairwise ("accounts merge" is exactly this). Exercise 5 asks for it.

Things that grow one piece at a time

A grid starts as water and cells turn into land one at a time; after each step, how many islands? A new land cell is a new island (+1). Then union it with each neighbor that's already land; every union that returns True glued two islands together (−1). Each step costs at most 4 unions. A BFS recount after every step would visit every land cell every time. That's the general reason to pick union-find over BFS: the links arrive over time, mixed in with the questions.

Go deeper: union-find that remembers a relation, not just "together"

Store a value on every parent pointer: how x relates to its parent. Then find can add up (or multiply) the values along the path, and compression folds them into one value from x straight to the root. Two classic uses:

  • "Evaluate division": you're told a / b = 2 and b / c = 3; what's a / c? Each pointer stores x / parent[x]. Multiply along the path to get x / root; then a / c = (a / root) ÷ (c / root) = 6.
  • Two teams, online: "a and b are on opposite teams" arrives as a stream; detect the first contradiction. Each pointer stores 0 (same team as parent) or 1 (opposite); add them up mod 2 along the path. This checks whether a graph can be split into two sides (bipartite) while edges are still arriving.

Count groups with a counter that drops by 1 on every successful union. A union that returns False means the edge closes a cycle (undirected graphs only). Map non-numeric items to numbers with a dict. Pick union-find over BFS when links arrive over time, mixed with questions.

You go through an undirected graph's edges, calling union(u, v) on each. For the edge (4, 9), union returns False. What does that tell you?

Right. False means find(4) == find(9) before the edge was added: some path of earlier edges already joins them. Add this edge and that path plus the edge is a loop. A duplicate edge is one way this happens (a loop of length 2), but far from the only one.

union returns False exactly when the two roots are the same, which means 4 and 9 are already in one group. Being in one group means some chain of earlier edges connects them, say 4–7, 7–2, 2–9. Now add 4–9: you can go 4 → 7 → 2 → 9 → 4, a cycle. A repeated edge is only the special case where the earlier chain is the edge itself. And "different components" is the opposite: that's when union returns True and merges them.

9Kruskal: the cheapest way to connect everything

Seven towns, and a list of possible cables between pairs of them, each with a cost. You want every town connected (directly or through others) for the lowest total cost. Cables that form a loop are wasted money: you could drop the most expensive cable on the loop and everyone would still be connected. So the answer has no loops: it's a tree that touches every town, a spanning tree, with n − 1 edges. The cheapest one is the minimum spanning tree (MST).

Joseph Kruskal's 1956 algorithm is greedy and short: sort the cables from cheapest to most expensive; take each cable unless its two towns are already connected. "Are these two already connected?", while connections keep being added, is exactly union-find's question, and union returning False is exactly "skip it, it would close a loop".

Kruskal on 7 towns. The cables are sorted by cost (the cells at the top). Yellow towns: the two ends of the cable being considered. Blue lines: cables taken. Green cells: taken; red: skipped because the ends were already connected. It stops after 6 = n − 1 cables.

Cost: sorting E edges is O(E log E), and the unions are O(E · α(n)), so the sort dominates. You can stop as soon as n − 1 edges are taken. Why is greedy safe here? If the cheapest remaining cable joins two groups, some cable must join those groups in any spanning tree, and none is cheaper; a skipped cable would close a loop in which every other cable is no more expensive. The full proof (the "cut" and "cycle" properties) is in topic 02.09, along with Prim's algorithm, which grows one tree outward from a start town instead of sorting every cable.

Go deeper: Kruskal in disguise
  • Clustering: stop Kruskal when k groups remain instead of 1, and you get the k clusters whose closest pair of points in different clusters is as far apart as possible. That's single-linkage clustering.
  • Maze generation: start with a grid of cells with every wall up. Visit the walls in random order and knock one down if the cells on its two sides aren't connected yet. The result is a maze with exactly one path between any two cells: a random spanning tree.
  • Libraries: NetworkX's Kruskal uses its own networkx.utils.UnionFind, and SciPy ships one as scipy.cluster.hierarchy.DisjointSet.

Kruskal's MST: sort edges by cost, take each edge whose ends are in different groups (union returns True), skip the rest. O(E log E) for the sort plus O(E · α(n)) for the unions.

Kruskal reaches the cable E–F, cost 8, and finds E and F already in the same group. Why is it right to skip it?

Right. The cables were sorted, so everything taken so far costs ≤ 8, and some of them already connect E to F. Adding E–F creates a loop, and a loop always has a cable you could drop, so it can't be in the cheapest tree (or it ties with one that is).

Remember the order: cables come cheapest first. So no later cable is cheaper than 8, and everything already taken costs 8 or less. Those taken cables already link E to F. Taking E–F too would make a loop: money spent on a connection that exists. And "the n − 1 cheapest cables" fails as soon as three cheap cables form a triangle: you'd buy a loop and leave some town unconnected. The skip rule is what prevents that.

10What union-find can't do

Union-find answers one question about groups that only merge, and it's very good at it. Interviewers like to push on the edges. Here's what falls outside, and what to reach for instead:

You need…Union-find?Use instead
"Same group?" while links keep arrivingYes, O(α(n)) amortized
All links known up front, one pass of questionsWorksBFS/DFS labeling, O(V + E), is just as good
Remove a link, or split a groupNoRebuild; or process offline in reverse; or a dynamic connectivity structure
The path between a and b, or its lengthNoBFS (topic 02.05)
One-way links: can a reach b?No (it treats links as two-way)DFS or BFS from a (topic 02.05)
List the members of a groupNot by itselfAdd a member list per root (merge smaller into bigger), or a circular "next" pointer per item, spliced in O(1) on union

Why there's no delete

Say a friendship between a and b ends. Are their groups now split? Not necessarily: some other chain of friendships may still connect them. Finding out means searching the remaining links, which is exactly the BFS we were avoiding. And the parent pointers can't help: after compression they don't record which link joined whom, only "my root is r". The forest remembers the answer, not the reasons for it.

What people do instead, cheapest idea first:

  • Rebuild from the current list of links now and then (say, nightly), if answers may be a little stale.
  • Go backwards in time if links only ever get removed and you know all the events in advance (offline). Start from the links still there at the end and read the events in reverse order: each removal becomes an addition, and union-find handles additions. Record each answer, then report them in the original order. This only works when the events are removals: if links are also added, reading backwards turns those additions into removals, and you're stuck again. Mixed adds and removals need the next idea.
  • Undo: union by size without compression changes at most two values per union, so you can push those on a stack and undo the most recent unions in O(1) each. (Compression is left out because it changes many pointers per find, and those would all need undoing; finds stay O(log n).) The offline algorithm for mixed adds and removals is built on this "rollback union-find": each link is alive during a known stretch of time, and a divide-and-conquer over time adds it on the way down and undoes it on the way back.
  • Fully dynamic connectivity structures support insert and delete in polylogarithmic time, a small power of log n (Holm, de Lichtenberg and Thorup, 2001: O(log² n) amortized per update). They're intricate and rarely worth it outside research code.
Go deeper: union-find on many machines, and where it runs in real systems

Many machines. Parent pointers assume one shared memory: a find on one machine that has to hop to another machine for every step is slow. Distributed systems usually use label propagation instead: every node starts with its own id as a label, and in each round takes the smallest label among itself and its neighbors, until nothing changes. The number of rounds is about the longest shortest path in the graph. Spark GraphX's connectedComponents works this way. A common hybrid: each machine runs union-find on its own share of the edges, then the small summaries (which local groups touch which) are merged.

Threads. Concurrent versions exist that update parent pointers with an atomic compare-and-swap (one hardware instruction that writes a new value only if the old one is still what the thread last saw) instead of a lock (Anderson and Woll, 1991).

Memory. Two integer arrays: with 4-byte integers, 10 million items take 80 MB. A plain Python list costs several times that (an 8-byte pointer per slot plus the integer objects), so for huge n, people use the array module or NumPy.

Where it runs. Kruskal's MST; image libraries labeling connected blobs of pixels (a first pass records "these two labels touch" in a union-find, a second pass relabels); physics simulations of percolation (the Hoshen-Kopelman algorithm); and type checkers that infer types, which merge type variables that must be equal. The forest of parent pointers was described by Bernard Galler and Michael Fischer in 1964, for a compiler problem: working out which variable names had been declared to share memory.

Union-find only merges. It can't delete a link, split a group, find a path or follow one-way edges. For deletions: rebuild, run offline in reverse (when links are only removed), or use a rollback union-find offline. When all links are known up front, BFS labeling is just as good; union-find wins when links arrive over time.

A social app shows "you're in the same friend network" badges. Friendships are added and removed all day. What do you tell the team?

Right. Union-find has no delete. Whether a removal splits a group depends on whether another path still connects them, which takes a search. A periodic rebuild (accepting slightly stale badges), offline tricks or a dynamic connectivity structure are the real options. Say which staleness the product can live with.

Picture the forest. Setting parent[x] = x cuts x out with everyone whose path ran through x, which can be thousands of people who are still friends with the rest. And x might still be connected through a different friend. Ignoring removals is a product bug: the badge would say two people are connected after their only link is gone. Union-find has no "un-union". You need to rebuild from the current friendships, or use a different structure.

11What to say in the interview

For "a log of friendships with timestamps; when was everyone first connected?", a strong answer:

"The groups only ever merge, and I need 'are they all one group yet?', so union-find. Items are the n people. I keep a counter of groups that starts at n. I sort the log by time and union each pair; every union that returns True merged two groups, so the counter drops by one. The first time it hits 1, that timestamp is the answer; if it never does, the answer is −1.

I'd use union by size, so no tree is ever more than log₂ n tall, and path compression, so repeated finds are about one step. Together that's O(α(n)) amortized per operation, effectively constant. Total: O(m log m) for the sort plus O(m · α(n)) for the unions, and O(n) memory. Running a BFS after every event would be O(m · (n + m)). If friendships could also end, plain union-find wouldn't work; I'd ask whether we can process the whole log offline, where a union-find with undo can handle both."

Habits that make you sound senior:

  • Name both fixes and what each one guarantees: size bounds the worst find at log₂ n, compression makes the average flat.
  • Say "amortized O(α(n))", and that α(n) ≤ 4 for any real input, rather than "O(1)".
  • Say why not BFS: links arrive over time, mixed with questions.
  • Say what it can't do (delete, paths, directed reachability) before you're asked.
  • Map strings to integers with a dict up front, and keep a component counter if anyone might ask "how many groups?".

✓What to remember

  1. Union-find keeps disjoint groups that only merge: union(a, b), find(x) (the group's representative, its root), connected(a, b) = find(a) == find(b).
  2. A label on every item makes union O(n). Parent pointers fix that: each group is a tree, union points one root at the other.
  3. Without a rule, trees become lines and find is O(n).
  4. Union by size (or rank): smaller root under bigger. An item gets deeper only when its group doubles, so depth ≤ log₂ n (23 for 10 million).
  5. Path compression: after a find, point the whole path at the root. Nobody changes group; sizes need no update.
  6. Both together: O(α(n)) amortized per operation, α(n) ≤ 4 in practice. A single find can still cost up to log₂ n.
  7. Patterns: a counter for the number of groups; union returning False means a cycle; a dict maps keys to numbers; Kruskal = sort edges + union.
  8. No delete, no paths, no one-way links. When all links are known up front, BFS is just as good.

Warm-up · pen and paper

1. Run it by hand

Eight items, 0 to 7. Use union by size where, on a tie, b's root goes under a's root, and full path compression in find. Call union(0, 1), union(2, 3), union(0, 2), union(4, 5), union(6, 7), union(4, 6), union(0, 4). Write parent after the last union. Which item is deepest, and how deep? Then call find(7): how many steps does it climb, what is parent afterwards, and how many steps would find(7) and find(6) take now?

Answer

union(0, 1): tie, 1 goes under 0. union(2, 3): 3 under 2. union(0, 2): sizes 2 and 2, tie, 2 under 0 (size 4). union(4, 5): 5 under 4. union(6, 7): 7 under 6. union(4, 6): tie, 6 under 4 (size 4). union(0, 4): sizes 4 and 4, tie, 4 under 0 (size 8). parent = [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 4, 4, 6].

The deepest item is 7: 7 → 6 → 4 → 0, depth 3 = log₂ 8, the most union by size allows. find(7) climbs 3 steps and points 7 and 6 at 0: parent = [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 4, 0, 0]. Now find(7) and find(6) take 1 step each. 3 and 5 weren't on the path, so they're still 2 steps from the root.

Warm-up · pen and paper

2. Break the plain version

Plain quick-union does parent[find(a)] = find(b). Give 6 unions on items 0 to 6 that build a single line 6 steps tall. Then say what union by size (ties: b's root under a's) builds from the same 6 calls, and how tall it is.

Answer

union(0, 1), union(1, 2), union(2, 3), union(3, 4), union(4, 5), union(5, 6). Each time, a is the current root (the last item added), and it goes under the newcomer b, giving 0 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6, 6 steps tall. (union(0, 1), union(0, 2), … from section 4 also works.) With union by size, every newcomer is a lone item, the smaller tree, so it goes under the big root: 1 to 6 all point at 0, a star 1 step tall.

Warm-up · pen and paper

3. Union-find or something else?

For each, say union-find (and what the items are), or what to use instead: (a) 50 million friendships arrive over a day, and after each one, "how many friend groups are there?"; (b) one fixed road map, and a single question, "can you drive from town A to town B?"; (c) users can follow and unfollow each other, and "is A connected to B?"; (d) "does package A depend on package B, directly or through other imports?"; (e) the cheapest set of fibre links that connects 2,000 data centers, out of 1.5 million possible links.

Answer

(a) Union-find over people, with a group counter: O(α(n)) per friendship. (b) BFS from A, O(V + E): one question about a fixed graph gains nothing from union-find. (c) Not plain union-find: unfollows are deletions. Rebuild periodically, process offline with a rollback union-find (reading in reverse alone won't do, since follows would become deletions), or search on demand. Following is also one-way, which union-find ignores. (d) Imports are one-way: DFS/BFS from A (or a topological order), not union-find. (e) Kruskal: sort 1.5 million links, union-find over 2,000 data centers, stop after 1,999 links. (Prim with a heap also works.)
