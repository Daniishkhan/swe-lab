Chapter 2 · Part 8

Shortest paths

P1 Dijkstra comes up often; Bellman-Ford and A* as follow-upsabout 60 minutes10 quick checks · 10 exercises

The last chapter's breadth-first search finds the route with the fewest edges. But a road that takes 1 minute and a road that takes 40 are both one edge. As soon as edges carry costs (minutes, euros, milliseconds of network delay), "fewest edges" stops meaning "cheapest", and you need a different tool. This chapter builds three of them from BFS, one fix at a time, and shows where each breaks.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Write Dijkstra's algorithm with a heap from memory, and explain why it is safe to call the closest unfinished node final.
  2. Handle stale heap entries ("lazy deletion"), give the cost O((V + E) log V), and say when the O(V²) version wins.
  3. Show with numbers why one negative edge breaks Dijkstra, and why adding a constant to every edge doesn't fix it.
  4. Write Bellman-Ford, explain its V − 1 rounds as dynamic programming, and use it to find negative cycles.
  5. Pick the faster special case: topological order for DAGs, a deque for 0/1 weights.
  6. Explain A*, what makes a heuristic admissible and consistent, and how maps and internet routers really find routes.

How to read this: top to bottom. It builds on Exploring graphs (BFS, the visited set, topological order) and uses a heap as a priority queue (The most urgent thing first): push and pop-smallest in O(log n). Section 8 links back to Dynamic programming. Answer each quick check before reading on.

1The problem: fewest roads isn't fastest

You build the router for a food delivery app. The city has 200,000 intersections and 500,000 road segments, and each segment has a travel time. Version one reuses last chapter's BFS. The first complaint arrives within the hour: from the pizza place to a customer, the app sent the rider through the city centre, 3 segments of 8, 9 and 12 minutes, 29 minutes in all. The ring road route has 6 segments of 2 to 4 minutes, 17 minutes in all. BFS did exactly what it promises. It found the fewest segments.

The next idea: list every route, add up its minutes, keep the smallest. But routes multiply. Even a 20 × 20 block grid has C(40, 20) ≈ 1.4 × 10¹¹ routes from one corner to the opposite one, and that counts only the routes that never turn back. At 10 million steps a second that's about 4 hours. For one pizza.

Both ideas fail for the same reason, seen from two sides. BFS throws away the minutes. Brute force keeps the minutes but forgets the lesson of BFS: once you know the best way to reach an intersection, every route through it can start from that best way. You never need the millions of other ways to get there.

We want both: count minutes like brute force, but finish each intersection once like BFS. That's Dijkstra's algorithm. Its one requirement, that no edge has a negative cost, leads to Bellman-Ford. Its one weakness, that it searches in every direction at once, leads to A*.

BFS sent the rider on the 3-segment, 29-minute route instead of the 6-segment, 17-minute one. What went wrong?

Right. BFS explores in rings of "number of edges", so the 3-segment route is reached first and kept. Minutes never enter its decisions. To get the fastest route the rings have to be measured in minutes, which is the change we make next.

Look at what BFS's queue sorts by: ring 1 is everything 1 edge away, ring 2 is 2 edges away, and so on. The 3-segment route is finished in ring 3, long before the 6-segment route appears in ring 6. Neighbour order only chooses among routes with the same number of edges, and running from both ends makes BFS faster, not smarter about minutes. BFS answers the question "fewest edges", correctly. It's the wrong question here.

2Rings measured in minutes

BFS works when every edge costs 1. So here is a first idea: make every edge cost 1. Chop a 7-minute road into 7 one-minute pieces, with a fake intersection between each pair. Now BFS's rings are minutes, and its first visit to a real intersection is the fastest route there.

real road:     A ───────── 7 min ───────── B

chopped:       A ─ · ─ · ─ · ─ · ─ · ─ · ─ B      7 one-minute edges, 6 fake nodes

It's correct, and it's hopeless. Our 500,000 segments average 2 minutes; in seconds, that's 120 pieces each and 60 million fake nodes. Worse, almost all the work is boring. Between two intersections, the search just walks along the road one second at a time. Nothing can branch off, nothing can be decided.

Skip the boring part

Picture the roads as fuses, each one as long as its travel time, knotted together at intersections. Light the fuse at the start. The flame runs along every fuse at the same speed and splits at each knot. The moment the flame reaches a knot is exactly the fastest travel time to that intersection, because the flame tries every route at once and the fastest one gets there first.

A computer can't simulate burning seconds one by one, but it doesn't need to. The only moments that matter are when the flame reaches a knot. So keep a list of "the flame will reach knot X at time t" for the knots the fire is heading to, and jump straight to the earliest one. When the flame reaches a knot, it starts down that knot's fuses, adding new arrival times to the list.

"Give me the smallest item, again and again, while new items keep arriving" is exactly what a priority queue does, and a binary heap does it in O(log n) per push or pop. Swap BFS's queue for a heap ordered by arrival time, and you have Dijkstra's algorithm (Edsger Dijkstra designed it in 1956, in about twenty minutes, sitting at a café in Amsterdam).

Dijkstra's algorithm is BFS with its first-in-first-out queue replaced by a priority queue ordered by total distance so far. It simulates a fire spreading along the edges at constant speed, jumping from one "the fire reaches a node" moment to the next.
Explain it another way: water in pipes

Pour water into a network of pipes at the start. Each pipe's length is its cost. Water flows at the same speed everywhere, and a junction gets wet at the moment of its shortest distance. Dijkstra's heap is the list of "which junction gets wet next". Where the picture breaks: a pipe can't have negative length. That's exactly the case Dijkstra can't handle (section 7).

3Dijkstra's algorithm, step by step

Three words first.

  • Tentative distance, dist[v]: the cheapest way to reach v found so far. It starts at 0 for the start and ∞ (infinity, "no route known") for everyone else, and it can only go down.
  • Relaxing an edge u → v of weight w: ask "is going through u better than the best route to v I know?" If dist[u] + w < dist[v], lower dist[v] to it. (The odd name is historical: the estimate for v "relaxes" toward the truth.)
  • Settled (or final): a node whose distance can't improve any more. In the fuse picture, the flame has reached it.

The algorithm: repeatedly take the unsettled node with the smallest tentative distance, call it settled, and relax every edge out of it. Step through it on a small one-way road map:

Dijkstra from S. The node being settled is highlighted, and the legend below the heap gives the colours for settled nodes and tentative ones. The heap is shown smallest first; greyed entries are stale (section 5). Watch A: it's first offered 4 (directly from S), then improved to 3 through B.

Notice the moment where A is improved from 4 to 3. A wasn't settled yet, so nothing was lost: a tentative distance is only a promise "at most this much". The settled ones never change. Here is the code:

import heapq, math

def dijkstra(graph, src):                   # graph[u] = [(v, w), ...], w >= 0
    dist = {u: math.inf for u in graph}
    dist[src] = 0
    heap = [(0, src)]                       # (tentative distance, node)
    while heap:
        d, u = heapq.heappop(heap)          # the closest node not yet handled
        if d > dist[u]:
            continue                        # stale entry: u was improved later
        for v, w in graph[u]:               # u is settled: relax its edges
            if d + w < dist[v]:
                dist[v] = d + w
                heapq.heappush(heap, (d + w, v))
    return dist

The line if d > dist[u]: continue is how this version tells settled nodes apart, and section 5 explains it. First, the question every interviewer asks.

4Why the closest node is safe to settle

Dijkstra is greedy: it makes a final decision (this node's distance is done) based only on what it has seen so far, and never takes it back. Greedy algorithms are often wrong, so this needs an argument. It's the part of the chapter to slow down on.

Take a moment from the player: S, B and A are settled (distances 0, 1, 3). The heap's smallest live entry is C at 4, via A. Could some route we haven't found yet reach C for less than 4?

      settled (final)            |   not settled yet
                                 |
   S(0) ─ ... ─ x  ──── w ─────► y ─ ... ─►  C
                                 |
    any route to C has to cross this line somewhere:
    x is its last settled node, y the first unsettled one

Every route to C starts at S, which is settled, and ends at C, which isn't. So it crosses the line at least once. Call x the last settled node before the first crossing and y the node just after it. Now follow three facts:

  1. When x was settled, it relaxed the edge x → y. x's own distance was already correct (it was settled earlier, by this same argument), so it was no more than this route's cost up to x. So y's tentative distance is at most "cost of this route up to y". Here: the route S → B → C crosses at B → C, so C's tentative was set to at most 1 + 5 = 6 back then.
  2. C was chosen as the smallest tentative distance, so tentative(C) ≤ tentative(y) ≤ cost of the route up to y.
  3. The rest of the route, from y on to C, costs at least 0, because no edge is negative. So the whole route costs at least what it cost up to y, which is at least tentative(C).

Every possible route to C costs at least 4. So 4 is the true distance, and settling C is safe. The argument works for whichever node is smallest, at every step, so every settled distance is correct.

Look at step 3 again, because that's where the whole algorithm leans on the weights. With a negative edge somewhere after y, the route could get cheaper after crossing the line, and "at least what it cost up to y" is false. That is the precise reason Dijkstra needs non-negative weights: not cycles, not zeros, only negatives.

Any route to the smallest unsettled node u must leave the settled region through some edge x → y, and y was already offered a distance no bigger than that route's cost so far. Since u is the smallest offer, and the rest of the route can't be negative, nothing beats u's offer. That one "can't be negative" step is why Dijkstra needs weights ≥ 0.
Explain it another way: the fire again

The flame reaches knots in order of time. When the flame first touches C, could another route reach C sooner? Then the flame, which runs down every route at once, would have arrived along it sooner. Taking the smallest entry in the heap is exactly "the next knot the flame touches". A negative edge would be a fuse that, as it burns, sends the flame back in time, and the order of arrivals stops meaning anything.

The proof says "the rest of the route, from y on, costs at least 0". Which kind of graph breaks exactly that step?

Right. A cycle with positive weights only makes routes longer, and a 0 edge keeps "at least 0" true. A negative edge after the crossing can make the route cheaper than its part up to y, so the smallest offer might not be final.

Put numbers on the step: the route reached y for 6, and C's offer is 4. If the rest of the route has edges 2 and 0, the total is 8 ≥ 4: fine. A cycle just adds more edges like those, so it only adds cost. Only an edge like −5 can bring 6 down to 3, below C's offer of 4. Zero-weight edges and cycles are both fine for Dijkstra; negative edges are not.

5Stale entries, and what it all costs

A textbook priority queue has a decrease-key operation: "A is now 3, not 4, move it up." Python's heapq doesn't, and finding A inside the heap would cost O(n) anyway. So we do the lazy thing: push a second entry (3, A) and leave the old (4, A) where it is. That's lazy deletion. The old entry is stale: when it eventually comes out, d = 4 but dist[A] = 3, so d > dist[u] and we skip it. In the player, those are the greyed heap cells.

Forget the skip, and the work explodes

Without the skip the answers stay right (a stale entry's distances are worse, so they never improve anything), but look at what it costs. Take 1,000 nodes that each, one after another, offer a hub a slightly better distance. The hub now sits in the heap 1,000 times. Say the hub has 1,000 roads out. Each of its 1,000 copies comes out and relaxes all 1,000 roads: 1,000,000 relaxations instead of 1,000. Exercise 4's tests build this graph (with 300 copies and 300 roads) and count your additions.

The cost

  1. Each node is settled once, and when it is, its edge list is read once: E relaxations in total.
  2. Each relaxation pushes at most one entry, so the heap holds at most E + 1 entries, and at most E + 1 pops happen.
  3. Each push and pop costs O(log E). Without parallel edges E ≤ V², so log E ≤ 2 log V, and that's O(log V). (With parallel edges, keep only the cheapest edge per pair first, or just write O(log E).)

Total: O((V + E) log V) time and O(V + E) memory. For our city, V + E is 700,000 and log₂ V is about 18: some 12 million basic heap steps, a second or two of Python. Compared with 10¹¹ routes, that's the win.

When the heap loses

Without a heap, you could find the next node by scanning all V tentative distances: V scans of V nodes, O(V²), plus E relaxations. For a road map that's terrible: 200,000² = 4 × 10¹⁰. But on a dense graph, where most pairs of nodes are connected and E is close to V², the heap version costs about V² log V, and the plain scan is the faster one.

Lazy deletion: instead of updating an entry in the heap, push a new one, and skip any popped entry whose distance is worse than the node's current best. Dijkstra with a binary heap costs O((V + E) log V). On a dense graph (E near V²) the heap-free O(V²) version wins.

A teammate deletes the line if d > dist[u]: continue "because the answers didn't change". What's true?

Right. A stale entry carries a distance worse than the node's best, so d + w is never better than what the neighbours already have. Nothing changes, and nothing loops, because no new entries get pushed. But the reading and adding happens anyway: the hub with 1,000 copies and 1,000 roads does a million useless relaxations.

Trace one stale pop. (4, A) comes out, but dist[A] is already 3. For each edge A → v of weight w, the code tries 4 + w, and dist[v] is already at most 3 + w, from when the live (3, A) entry was handled. So no distance changes and no entry is pushed: the answers stay right and the heap still empties. The harm is purely wasted work, and it multiplies: copies × out-edges.

5,000 cities, and a flight between every pair: E ≈ 25 million. Which Dijkstra do you write?

Right. Here E is about V², so the E relaxations happen in both versions; the difference is that the heap version also pays log V ≈ 12 per push. The plain scan costs V per settled node, V² in all, which is no more than the edges you have to read anyway.

Plug in. Heap: (5,000 + 25,000,000) × log₂ 5,000 ≈ 25 million × 12 ≈ 3 × 10⁸. Scan: 5,000 × 5,000 = 2.5 × 10⁷. "Log V is small" stops being true when it multiplies V². Bellman-Ford is slower still, O(V × E) ≈ 10¹¹; density never stops Dijkstra working, only changes which version is fastest.

Go deeper: other priority queues

With a Fibonacci heap, decrease-key costs O(1) amortized and Dijkstra becomes O(E + V log V). For decades that was the best bound known; in 2025 Duan and coauthors beat it for directed graphs, O(E log2/3 V), with an algorithm that mixes Dijkstra and Bellman-Ford ideas. Neither is used in practice: the constant factors are bad, and binary heaps with lazy deletion (or pairing heaps) win on real graphs. When weights are small integers (say 1 to C), Dial's algorithm keeps one bucket per distance value and walks through them in order: O(V × C + E), no heap at all. Radix heaps push that idea further. The 0-1 BFS in section 9 is the C = 1 case.

6One destination: stopping early, and the route itself

The rider needs one route to one customer, not distances to 200,000 intersections. You can stop early, but only at the right moment. A node's distance is final when it is settled, which is when it is popped. Being pushed, or "discovered", is only an offer.

0 → 3 costs 10;   0 → 1 → 2 → 3 costs 1 + 1 + 1 = 3

pop 0:  offers 3 a distance of 10, and 1 a distance of 1
           ↑ stop here ("3 discovered!") and you answer 10
pop 1, pop 2:  2 offers 3 a distance of 3
pop 3:  3 is settled at 3        ← stop here: the right answer

To return the route, record where each improvement came from: whenever dist[v] drops, set parent[v] = u. When the goal is popped, walk the parents back to the start and reverse, exactly as in BFS. The last improvement wins, so the parent chain is a cheapest route.

On that graph, a shortest_route returns as soon as the goal 3 gets pushed onto the heap. What does it answer?

Right. Popping 0 relaxes 0 → 3 straight away, so 3 is discovered with 10 while 1 and 2 are still waiting. Only the pop order is sorted by distance. Stop when the goal is popped, and the cut-line proof from section 4 guarantees the answer.

Step it. The first pop is 0, and it relaxes all its edges at once: 3 gets the offer 10 and 1 gets 1. If you stop at "3 has a distance", you answer 10, whatever the edge order. The cheap route needs pops of 1 and 2 first. "First found is cheapest" is true for the order nodes come out of the heap, not the order they go in. On a big map this matters a lot: stopping at the pop also stops the search early, after settling only the nodes closer than the goal.

Go deeper: bidirectional Dijkstra

For one start and one goal, run Dijkstra forward from the start and backward from the goal (on reversed edges), alternating. On a road map each search covers a disc, and two discs of radius r/2 have half the area of one disc of radius r. The stopping rule is subtle: when the two searches settle a common node, the best route found so far is not necessarily through that node. Keep the best dist_f[u] + w + dist_b[v] over every edge u → v seen between the two sides, and stop when the smallest keys of the two heaps add up to at least that best.

7Negative edges break Dijkstra

Negative weights sound odd for roads, but they turn up in real problems. An electric car going downhill gains charge. A delivery leg can include a refund. In currency trading, section 8 turns exchange rates into weights that are negative whenever a trade gains value. And scheduling problems written as "this must start at least 3 minutes before that" become graphs with negative edges.

Go back to the player in section 3 and tick the box that adds D → C with weight −6. Dijkstra settles C at 4 early on. Much later, it settles D at 9 and sees D → C: 9 − 6 = 3, cheaper than 4. But C is settled, and settled means final: Dijkstra never looks back. It reports C = 4 and T = 7. The truth is C = 3 (S → B → D → C) and T = 6. The proof's step 3 failed: the route S → B → D → C crossed the line at B → D, already costing 9, and then got cheaper.

The tempting fix that doesn't work

"Add a big constant to every edge so they're all ≥ 0, run Dijkstra, subtract it afterwards." Try it on three nodes:

edges:   0 → 1  costs 2           0 → 2  costs 5          2 → 1  costs −4

routes to 1:    0 → 1           1 edge     2
                0 → 2 → 1       2 edges    5 − 4 = 1     ← the cheapest

add 4 to every edge:
                0 → 1           1 edge     6             ← now this one looks cheapest
                0 → 2 → 1       2 edges    9 + 0 = 9

Adding 4 to every edge adds 4 to a 1-edge route but 8 to a 2-edge route. Routes with more edges are punished more, so the order of routes changes. (There is a correct reweighting, but it adds a different amount per node, not per edge, and computing it takes Bellman-Ford: see the "Go deeper" box below.)

With a negative edge, a route can get cheaper after it leaves the settled region, so "smallest offer is final" is no longer true, and Dijkstra can return wrong distances. Adding a constant to every edge doesn't help: it penalizes routes by their number of edges.

On the three-node graph, you add 4 to every edge, run Dijkstra, and then subtract 4 × (the number of edges on the route it found). What distance do you report for node 1?

Right. Undoing the shift correctly needs the right route first, and the shift changed which route looks cheapest. With the shift, a k-edge route costs its real cost plus 4k, so it compares routes of different lengths unfairly.

The subtraction happens after Dijkstra has already chosen. In the shifted graph, 0 → 1 costs 6 and 0 → 2 → 1 costs 9 + 0 = 9, so Dijkstra picks 0 → 1. Undo its shift: 6 − 4 = 2. But the real cheapest is 1, along the route Dijkstra rejected. All weights are now ≥ 0, so Dijkstra itself runs fine: it just answers a different question.

Go deeper: Dijkstra without a "settled" rule, and Johnson's reweighting

The lazy code in section 3 doesn't actually forbid improving a node after it was popped: with D → C = −6 it would lower dist[C] to 3 and push C again, and here it would even end with the right answers. In general, with negative edges but no negative cycle, that version still ends correct, but a node can be re-popped many times, and there are graphs where that takes exponential time. You lose the guarantee that made Dijkstra fast.

Johnson's algorithm does reweighting properly, for all-pairs shortest paths on graphs with negative edges. Run Bellman-Ford once to get a number h(v) for every node, then use w'(u, v) = w(u, v) + h(u) − h(v). Along any route from s to t the h terms cancel, leaving the true cost + h(s) − h(t), the same shift for every route between those two nodes. So the order of routes is unchanged, and the choice of h makes every w' ≥ 0. Then Dijkstra from each node: O(V × E log V) in total, much better than V runs of Bellman-Ford on sparse graphs.

8Bellman-Ford: relax everything, again

Dijkstra's speed came from knowing, at each step, which node is final. With negative edges we can't know that. So give up on it. Relax every edge, in any order. Then do it again. And again. Each pass is a round. Distances only ever go down, and each one stays an honest "there's a route at least this good". The question is when they're all right.

Round k fixes every route of k edges

Claim: after round k, dist[v] is at most the cost of the cheapest route to v that uses at most k edges. Round 0, before any relaxing: only the start has distance 0, true for routes of 0 edges. Now suppose the claim holds after round k − 1, and take the cheapest route to v with at most k edges, ending with the edge u → v. Everything before that last edge is a route to u with at most k − 1 edges, so after round k − 1, dist[u] is at most its cost. Round k relaxes u → v, so dist[v] drops to at most that plus w. Done.

A cheapest route never needs to visit a node twice (without negative cycles, a loop can only add cost or nothing), so it has at most V nodes and V − 1 edges. After V − 1 rounds, every distance is right. That gives O(V × E) time. And if a whole round changes nothing, the next round would see exactly the same numbers, so you can stop early.

Bellman-Ford from S. Every round relaxes the edges in the same, deliberately unlucky, order: C→D, B→C, A→D, A→B, S→C, S→A (and D→B last, when ticked). The table has one row per round. Watch the right answer creep forward one edge per round. Then tick the box: round 5 still lowers a distance.
import math

def bellman_ford(n, edges, src):              # edges: (u, v, w), w may be < 0
    dist = [math.inf] * n
    dist[src] = 0
    for _ in range(n - 1):                    # a cheapest route has <= n - 1 edges
        changed = False
        for u, v, w in edges:
            if dist[u] + w < dist[v]:         # inf + w stays inf, so this is safe
                dist[v] = dist[u] + w
                changed = True
        if not changed:
            break                             # a quiet round: nothing will change again
    return dist

It's dynamic programming

Remember the DP recipe: define a state, write how it's built from smaller states, fill a table. Here the state is "cheapest route to v using at most k edges":

best[k][v] = min( best[k−1][v],                                  (don't use the k-th edge)
                  min over edges u → v of  best[k−1][u] + w(u, v) )  (end with u → v)

best[0][src] = 0,  best[0][v] = ∞ for everyone else.   Answer: best[V−1][v].

The figure's table is exactly this DP table, one row per k. Bellman-Ford keeps only one row and updates it in place. In-place updates can only run ahead of the table (a value lowered early in round k can be used later in the same round), never behind, so the proof still holds. Fittingly, Richard Bellman is the same Bellman who named dynamic programming.

Why does Bellman-Ford run exactly V − 1 rounds?

Right. A cheapest route visits each node at most once, so it has at most V nodes and V − 1 edges; by round V − 1, every such route has been "replayed" edge by edge. Many graphs do finish sooner, which is why you stop after a quiet round, but V − 1 is the worst case: the figure's chain needed all 4 rounds.

A round isn't tied to one node: one round can fix many nodes, or none. Think of it per route instead. In the figure, the cheapest route to D is S → A → B → C → D, 4 edges, and the edge order is chosen so that each round extends it by exactly one edge: A in round 1, B in round 2, C in round 3, D in round 4. A route can't have more than V − 1 = 4 edges without repeating a node, so 4 rounds always suffice. "About log V" is wrong in the worst case: a chain of 1,000 nodes (999 edges) listed backwards needs all 999 rounds.

Negative cycles

A negative cycle is a loop whose weights add up to less than 0, like B → C → D → B in the figure with the box ticked: 2 − 2 − 1 = −1. Going round it once more always saves another 1, so there is no cheapest route to anything the cycle can reach: the answer is "−∞". Bellman-Ford can tell. Without a negative cycle, nothing changes after round V − 1. So run one more round: if anything still improves, a negative cycle can be reached from the start.

A trap with undirected graphs: an undirected edge of weight −2 is a road you can drive both ways, so A → B → A is already a cycle costing −4. Any negative edge in an undirected graph is a negative cycle, and "shortest path" stops making sense; such problems need different tools.

Two details interviewers probe. The node that improves in round V may not be on the cycle; it can be downstream of it. And a negative cycle the start can't reach never shows up, and doesn't matter for that start's distances. To find any negative cycle in the whole graph, start every node at distance 0 (as if an extra node had a 0-cost edge to everyone) and run the same thing. To print the cycle, keep parent pointers; from a node that improved in round V, walk back V parents, which is guaranteed to land you on the cycle, then keep walking until you return to that node. Exercise 7.

Explain it another way: currency arbitrage

Exchange rates: 1 USD buys 0.9 EUR, 1 EUR buys 0.9 GBP, 1 GBP buys 1.25 USD. Go round: 0.9 × 0.9 × 1.25 = 1.0125. Every lap earns 1.25%. That's arbitrage, and finding it is a negative-cycle search. Products turn into sums with logarithms, and "more than 1" turns into "less than 0" with a minus sign: give each trade the weight −log(rate). A lap's weights add up to −log(product of rates), which is negative exactly when the product is above 1. Here −log(1.0125) ≈ −0.0124: a negative cycle. Real trading desks have done this, though fees and speed close such gaps within milliseconds.

Bellman-Ford relaxes every edge, V − 1 times. Round k accounts for every route of up to k edges, and a cheapest route has at most V − 1. It costs O(V × E), works with negative edges, stops early after a quiet round, and finds negative cycles: if round V still improves something, one is reachable.

You run a V-th round, and it still lowers dist[X]. What do you know?

Right. Without a reachable negative cycle, all distances are final after V − 1 rounds, so any improvement proves one exists. But the cycle keeps lowering everything downstream of it too, and X may simply be one of those. That's why the cycle-printing trick first walks back V parent steps.

Picture the cycle B → C → D → B costing −1, plus an edge D → E. Every lap lowers D, and so E as well. In round V, E might be the one that improves, and E isn't on the cycle. More rounds never finish the job either: each lap saves another 1, forever. A negative edge alone (without a cycle) is fine; after V − 1 rounds those distances are all final.

9Special shapes that are faster: DAGs and 0/1 weights

Dijkstra pays log V per step to find the next node in order; Bellman-Ford pays V rounds because it doesn't know the order. Sometimes the order is free.

No cycles: relax in topological order

In a DAG (a directed graph with no cycles), a topological order (last chapter) lists every node after everything that leads into it. Go through the nodes in that order and relax each one's outgoing edges. When you reach a node, every edge into it has already been relaxed, from nodes whose distances were already final, so its distance is final too. One pass: O(V + E), and negative edges are fine, because there's no cycle to exploit.

topological order:   S   A   B   C   T
relax S's edges, then A's, then B's, ... each edge exactly once

This is the same thing as the DP chapter's "DP is a shortest path on a DAG": the states are nodes, the fill order is the topological order. Flip the signs, or use max instead of min, and you get the longest path in a DAG: the critical path of a project or a build, the chain of tasks that sets the earliest finish time. (Longest simple paths in graphs with cycles are NP-hard: no fast algorithm is known.)

Weights of only 0 and 1: a deque

Some grid puzzles have moves that are free and moves that cost 1 (breaking a wall, changing direction). BFS's queue worked because it only ever held two distances, d and d + 1. Keep that shape with a double-ended queue: when you relax a 0 edge, push the neighbour on the front (same distance as the current node); a 1 edge goes on the back. The deque stays sorted, and this 0-1 BFS runs in O(V + E). A node can be pushed more than once (at most twice: first at d + 1, then at d), much like Dijkstra's stale entries.

import math
from collections import deque

def zero_one_bfs(graph, src):                 # graph[u] = [(v, w)], w is 0 or 1
    dist = {u: math.inf for u in graph}
    dist[src] = 0
    dq = deque([src])
    while dq:
        u = dq.popleft()
        for v, w in graph[u]:
            if dist[u] + w < dist[v]:
                dist[v] = dist[u] + w
                (dq.appendleft if w == 0 else dq.append)(v)   # 0: front, 1: back
    return dist

This version has no stale check: a node that comes out a second time re-reads its edges but can't improve anything. Since each node is pushed at most twice, that at most doubles the work, which stays O(V + E).

Pick the cheapest tool the graph allows. No cycles: relax in topological order, O(V + E), negative edges allowed. Weights 0 or 1: a deque, 0 edges to the front, O(V + E). All weights equal: plain BFS. General weights ≥ 0: Dijkstra. Negative weights: Bellman-Ford.

10A*: aim the search at the goal

Dijkstra has one more waste, and it's huge on maps. To route from Amsterdam to Paris, it settles every node closer to Amsterdam than Paris is: all of the Netherlands and Belgium, but also a large part of Germany, which is the wrong direction entirely. The fire spreads in a circle, and the goal is in one direction.

Numbers on an open grid: with the goal 189 steps to the right, Dijkstra settles every cell within 189 steps, a diamond of about 2 × 189² ≈ 71,000 cells. The actual route is 190 cells.

Add a guess of what's left

Dijkstra orders the heap by g(v): the cost from the start to v, known. Add h(v), a heuristic: a quick estimate of the cost from v to the goal, which you can compute without searching, such as the straight-line distance. Order the heap by

f(v) = g(v) + h(v)       = an estimate of the whole trip, start → v → goal

Nodes in the direction of the goal have small h, so they come out first. The fire now burns faster toward the goal. That's A* ("A star"; Hart, Nilsson and Raphael, 1968, for the Shakey robot). Everything else is Dijkstra: same heap, same relaxing, same stale-entry skip. With h = 0 it is Dijkstra.

When is A*'s answer still the cheapest?

The heuristic must be admissible: it never overestimates the true remaining cost. On a 4-direction grid where every step costs at least 1, the Manhattan distance |Δrow| + |Δcol| is admissible: you can't get there in fewer steps than that, walls or not. On roads, straight-line distance divided by the top speed limit is admissible for travel time.

Why that's enough: suppose A* is about to pop the goal with a cost g that's too big, while the cheapest route costs C < g. Until the goal is popped, some node n of that cheapest route sits in the heap with g(n) equal to its cost along the route. (At first that's the start. When such a node is popped, it relaxes the next edge of the route and hands the role on.) For that node, f(n) = g(n) + h(n) ≤ g(n) + (true cost from n to the goal) = C, because h doesn't overestimate. So f(n) ≤ C < g = f(goal): n comes out first, not the goal. This repeats until the goal comes out along the cheapest route, at cost C.

A stronger condition is consistent (or monotone): for every edge u → v, h(u) ≤ w(u, v) + h(v), and h(goal) = 0. In words, the estimate from u is never more than one step to v plus the estimate from v. (It's the triangle inequality: a side of a triangle is never longer than the other two together.) Then f never decreases along a route, and each node is settled once, just as in Dijkstra. In fact, A* with a consistent h is exactly Dijkstra on the edge weights w − h(u) + h(v), which are all ≥ 0. Manhattan on a grid and straight-line distance on roads are both consistent. Most heuristics people write are; an admissible heuristic that isn't consistent needs A* to reopen settled nodes.

HeuristicWhat happens
h = 0Dijkstra: correct, searches in every direction

A* is Dijkstra ordered by f = g + h, where h estimates the remaining cost. If h never overestimates (admissible), the answer is still the cheapest; if it also obeys h(u) ≤ w(u, v) + h(v) (consistent), every node is settled at most once. A better h settles fewer nodes. h = 0 is Dijkstra.

A map grid where each step costs 1 to 9 (mud costs more). To make A* faster, you use h = 9 × Manhattan distance. What happens?

Right. If the last 10 cells are cheap road, the true remaining cost is 10, but h says 90. A good route through those cells looks expensive and waits, while a worse route with a smaller f reaches the goal first. The admissible choice here is 1 × Manhattan, using the cheapest step cost.

The goal still comes out of the heap, so A* ends. But check the promise "h never overestimates": 10 steps of road cost 10, and 9 × Manhattan says 90. A* then believes a route through the road is 80 worse than it is, and it may let a muddier route reach the goal first, with a higher real cost. The safe rule: multiply Manhattan distance by the cheapest possible step, here 1.

Go deeper: heuristics for other moves, and ties
  • 8 directions (diagonals allowed at cost √2): Manhattan overestimates, since one diagonal step covers both a row and a column. Use the octile distance: max(dx, dy) + (√2 − 1) × min(dx, dy). With diagonals costing 1, use the Chebyshev distance max(dx, dy).
  • Ties. On an open grid with the goal on a diagonal, thousands of cells have the same f. Breaking ties toward the larger g (the cell further along) makes A* run straight at the goal instead of filling the rectangle between start and goal.
  • Memory. A* keeps everything it touched. IDA* (iterative deepening A*) trades time for memory, the way iterative deepening does for DFS. Games use precomputed waypoint graphs or navigation meshes so the search runs on hundreds of nodes, not millions of cells.

11Real systems: maps and the internet

Maps

The road network of Western Europe, as used in research benchmarks, has about 18 million nodes and 42 million edges. Plain Dijkstra across it takes a few seconds, far too slow for a server answering thousands of route requests a second. Production route planners use the ideas in this chapter plus preprocessing:

  • Bidirectional search (section 6's aside) roughly halves the area searched.
  • A* with landmarks (ALT). Precompute the distances from a dozen landmarks to every node. The triangle inequality turns them into an admissible heuristic much sharper than straight-line distance, which ignores that roads curve.
  • Contraction hierarchies. Offline, rank the nodes by importance (motorway junctions high, cul-de-sacs low) and add "shortcut" edges that skip unimportant nodes. A query then runs a bidirectional Dijkstra that only climbs to more important nodes, and settles a few hundred nodes instead of millions: well under a millisecond. Open-source routers such as OSRM work this way.
  • Live traffic changes the weights every few minutes, and rebuilding all shortcuts takes too long. Customizable route planning splits the work: the expensive structure depends only on the road layout, and a fast "customization" step recomputes it for new travel times in seconds.

The internet: two families of routing

Routers inside one network (a company, a provider) choose paths with one of two designs, and they are this chapter's two algorithms.

  • Link-state (OSPF, IS-IS). Every router floods a description of its own links to all the others, so every router holds the whole map, and runs Dijkstra on it (OSPF literally calls this the "shortest path first" calculation). When a link fails, the news floods out, everyone reruns Dijkstra, and they all agree within seconds. Cost: memory and CPU for the full map.
  • Distance-vector (RIP). A router only tells its neighbours "here's how far I am from every destination". Each router relaxes: my distance to X is the minimum over neighbours of (link cost + their distance to X). That's Bellman-Ford, spread over many machines, with each exchange of messages acting as one round. Cheap and simple, but bad news travels slowly. If a destination disappears, two routers can keep "learning" a route from each other, raising their costs one step per exchange: count to infinity. RIP limits this by treating 16 hops as infinity, which is also why RIP networks can't be more than 15 hops across.
  • BGP, between the networks that make up the internet, is path-vector: like distance-vector, but each announcement carries the whole list of networks on the path, so a router can see and refuse a loop. It also picks routes by business policy, not only by length.

A destination network goes offline. Why can RIP routers take many rounds to agree it's unreachable, while OSPF routers agree within seconds?

Right. Router A says "I'm 3 hops away", B believes it and says "4", A's old route died so it now believes B and says "5", and so on until 16 means infinity. An OSPF router learns directly that the link is down (it has the whole map) and reruns Dijkstra on the new map.

It's the other way round: OSPF (link-state) runs Dijkstra, and RIP (distance-vector) is Bellman-Ford spread over routers. The slowness is about information, not CPU. A RIP router only hears numbers, "my distance to X is 3", without the route behind them, so it can't tell that neighbour's route ran through itself. Two routers keep raising each other by one per exchange, up to RIP's 16 = infinity. OSPF doesn't store every path either: it stores the map, and reruns Dijkstra when the map changes.

12What to say in the interview

When a weighted graph shows up, a strong answer sounds like this:

  1. Name the graph and the weights: "Cities are nodes, flights are directed edges, and the weight is the price. Prices are never negative."
  2. Pick the tool and say why: "Weights differ, so BFS's fewest edges isn't cheapest. Non-negative, so Dijkstra: the closest unsettled node is final, because any other route would have to leave the settled set through an edge whose offer is already at least as big, and the rest can't be negative." If weights can be negative: "Bellman-Ford, V − 1 rounds, and a V-th round to detect negative cycles."
  3. Say the details that catch bugs: "heapq has no decrease-key, so I push duplicates and skip a popped entry whose distance is worse than the best. For one target I stop when it's popped, not when it's pushed, and I keep parents to rebuild the route."
  4. Say the cost: "O((V + E) log V) time, O(V + E) memory. If the graph is dense I'd use the O(V²) array version."
  5. Offer the follow-ups: "For one target on a map, A* with an admissible heuristic like straight-line distance. If it's a DAG, one pass in topological order. At map scale, preprocessing like contraction hierarchies."
Go deeper: follow-up questions interviewers like
  • "Cheapest flight with at most K stops." Dijkstra's "settle once" breaks: a cheaper route with too many stops can block a dearer one with few. Bellman-Ford with K + 1 rounds, copying the distance row before each round so a round adds only one edge, is the clean answer (the DP table of section 8, row by row). Or Dijkstra over states (node, stops used).
  • "Distances between every pair of nodes." V runs of Dijkstra, O(V × E log V), for sparse graphs. For small dense graphs, Floyd-Warshall is a 4-line DP: for each k, for each i, for each j, d[i][j] = min(d[i][j], d[i][k] + d[k][j]). Once the outer loop has finished k, d[i][j] is the cheapest route whose in-between nodes are all among 0..k: a DP over "which nodes may be used as stops". O(V³), negative edges allowed, and a negative d[i][i] means a negative cycle.
  • "Network delay time": when does a signal reach every node? Dijkstra from the source, then the largest distance; −1 if any node is unreachable.
  • "Path with maximum probability." Multiply probabilities: use −log(p) as weights (all ≥ 0 since p ≤ 1), or run Dijkstra on the products directly: max instead of min, × instead of +. It works because multiplying by p ≤ 1 never makes a route more likely.
  • "Minimum effort path" (the cost of a route is its single worst step). Dijkstra still works with max instead of plus, because extending a route never makes its cost go down. So does binary search on the answer plus BFS.
  • "What if the weights change constantly?" Recompute from scratch if it's fast enough; otherwise incremental algorithms (D* Lite in robotics) or the customizable route planning split from section 11.

✓What to remember

  1. BFS finds fewest edges. With costs on edges you need distance-ordered search: Dijkstra is BFS with a heap keyed on distance so far.
  2. Dijkstra settles the closest unsettled node and relaxes its edges. It's safe because any other route must leave the settled set through an edge already offered, and the rest of the route can't be negative.
  3. Lazy deletion: push duplicates, skip stale pops (d > dist[u]). Without the skip, answers stay right but work multiplies. Cost O((V + E) log V); O(V²) without a heap on dense graphs.
  4. For one target, stop when it is popped, not pushed. Parents rebuild the route.
  5. A negative edge breaks Dijkstra. Adding a constant to every edge penalizes routes with more edges; it's not a fix.
  6. Bellman-Ford: relax all edges V − 1 times, O(V × E). Round k covers routes of k edges; it's DP over "edges allowed". Stop after a quiet round. A V-th round that improves anything means a reachable negative cycle.
  7. DAG: one pass in topological order, O(V + E), negatives fine. 0/1 weights: deque, O(V + E).
  8. A*: order by g + h. Admissible h (never overestimates) keeps the answer optimal; consistent h settles each node once; h = 0 is Dijkstra. Maps add preprocessing; OSPF is Dijkstra on every router, RIP is distributed Bellman-Ford.

Warm-up · pen and paper

1. Trace Dijkstra and Bellman-Ford by hand

Directed edges: S→A 2, S→B 6, A→B 3, A→C 7, B→C 1, B→D 5, C→D 1. (a) Run Dijkstra from S. Write the heap after each pop, marking stale entries, and the order nodes are settled. (b) Run Bellman-Ford with the edges in exactly the order listed. How many rounds change something? Now list them in reverse order and count again.

Answers

(a) Pop S(0): push A2, B6. Heap [A2, B6]. Pop A(2): B improves to 5, C gets 9. Heap [B5, B6, C9]. Pop B(5): C improves to 6, D gets 10. Heap [B6 stale, C6, C9, D10]. Pop (6, B): stale, skip (it ties with (6, C); either can come out first). Pop C(6): D improves to 7. Heap [D7, C9 stale, D10 stale]. Pop D(7). Then (9, C) and (10, D) come out stale. Settled in the order S, A, B, C, D, at 0, 2, 5, 6, 7.

(b) In the listed order, every edge comes after the edges into its start node, so round 1 already finds every final distance and round 2 changes nothing: 1 round with changes. Reversed (C→D, B→D, B→C, A→C, A→B, S→B, S→A): round 1 sets only B = 6 and A = 2 (S's edges come last). Round 2: B→D gives D = 11, B→C gives C = 7, A→C's 9 is no better, A→B gives B = 5. Round 3: C→D gives D = 8, B→D gives 10 (no better than 8), B→C gives C = 6. Round 4: C→D gives D = 7. Round 5: nothing. 4 rounds with changes, the worst case for V = 5. Edge order changes the speed, never the answer.

Warm-up · pen and paper

2. Pick the tool

Name the nodes, edges and weights, then the algorithm and its cost. (a) Fewest moves for a knight on a chessboard. (b) Cheapest train ticket route across Europe. (c) In a grid, moving along the arrow printed in a cell is free, and changing a cell's arrow costs 1: the fewest changes to get from the top left to the bottom right. (d) The earliest time a build of 3,000 tasks can finish, given task durations and dependencies. (e) Is there a sequence of currency trades that ends with more money than it started? (f) A game character finding a path on a 2,000 × 2,000 map.

Answers

(a) Squares and moves, all cost 1: BFS. (b) Stations, trains, ticket prices ≥ 0: Dijkstra with a heap. (c) Cells, moves to neighbours, cost 0 along the arrow and 1 otherwise: 0-1 BFS, O(cells). (d) Tasks with dependency arrows, weighted by duration: the longest path in a DAG, one pass in topological order (the critical path). (e) Currencies, trades, weight −log(rate): Bellman-Ford's negative-cycle check, run from everyone at distance 0. (f) Cells, moves, step costs: A* with Manhattan or octile distance, depending on whether diagonals are allowed.

Warm-up · pen and paper

3. Break the naive versions

(a) Draw the smallest graph you can, with one negative edge and no negative cycle, where Dijkstra (with a settled set) returns a wrong distance. (b) Draw a graph where stopping at "goal pushed" gives the wrong answer but stopping at "goal popped" is right. (c) Steps cost 1 or 9, and A* uses h = 9 × Manhattan distance. Find a map of 2 rows and 3 columns where it returns a route that costs more than the cheapest one.

Answers

(a) Three nodes: S→A 2, S→B 5, B→A −4. Dijkstra settles A at 2 before B (5) is popped, then can't use B→A to give A its true distance 1. (b) S→G 10, S→X 1, X→G 1: G is pushed first at 10, but popped at 2. (c) Rows "191" and "111", from the top left to the top right. The cheapest route goes down, along and up: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4. A* starts at the top left: the mud cell gets f = 9 + 9 × 1 = 18, the cell below gets f = 1 + 9 × 3 = 28. So A* walks through the mud, pushes the goal with g = 9 + 1 = 10, f = 10, and pops it long before the f = 28 cell. It answers 10. The true remaining cost below was 3, and h claimed 27.

Distances, or None if a negative cycle is reachable from src. 400 small random graphs are checked against a brute force over every simple path and cycle.
