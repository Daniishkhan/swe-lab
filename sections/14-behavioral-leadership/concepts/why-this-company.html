Why this company: Netflix and Anthropic

P1 asked in the first recruiter call and again onsiteabout 25 minutes, plus 90 minutes reading and writing7 quick checks · 5 exercises

"Why do you want to work here?" sounds like small talk. At Netflix and Anthropic it isn't. Both companies write down, in public, how they expect people to work, and both screen for it. This chapter shows how to read those documents, what they say, and how to turn them into a 90-second answer that's specific, true, and tied to something you've done.

By the end you'll be able to

  1. Say what "why us?" is really testing, and why "I love the product" fails.
  2. Read a culture document as a list of behaviors, and match each to evidence from your own work.
  3. Name the main ideas in Netflix's culture memo and in Anthropic's mission, values, Core Views on AI Safety and Responsible Scaling Policy.
  4. Give a 90-second "why Netflix" and "why Anthropic", each tying two values to a story.
  5. Ask recruiters the questions that tell you how to prepare.

How to read this: top to bottom. It uses the stories and the "I vs we" habit from Your project deep dive. Company documents change: this chapter was checked in November 2026, so read the live versions linked in each section before you write your answers, and trust them over this page.

1The problem: an answer anyone could give

A recruiter at a streaming company talks to maybe 15 candidates a week. She asks each one, "Why us?" Twelve say some version of: "I love the product, I watch it every night, and I'd like to work at scale." She writes "generic" next to all twelve.

The other three say things like: "Your culture memo says you share context instead of setting rules. On my last team I replaced a 40-step release checklist with a dashboard that showed people the risk, and incidents went down. I want to work somewhere that's the default." She writes that one down word for word.

The twelve answers aren't wrong. They're just interchangeable: you could swap the company name and they'd still work. An answer you could give to any company tells the listener nothing about you or about why you chose them.

What makes the strong answer strong?

Right: a specific idea from them, plus evidence from you. That's the formula for the whole chapter.

Try the swap test: replace the company's name with a competitor's. If the answer still works, it's generic. Flattery passes the swap test ("you have a great culture" fits anywhere), and so does reciting the memo, which tells them you read it but not that you live it. The strong answer fails the swap test, because it's tied to one company's idea, and it's believable, because it comes with a real story.

2What "why us?" is really testing

Think of it like being asked "why me?" on a third date. The other person isn't fishing for compliments. They want to know three things: did you pay attention, do you want the same things, and are you choosing them or just choosing someone. Interviewers want the same three:

  1. Homework: do you know what this company is and how it works?
  2. Fit: does the way you already work match the way they want people to work? This is the big one. Both companies believe their culture is a reason they win, and a person who'd fight it is expensive.
  3. Intent: are you choosing them on purpose? A candidate who has a reason will probably accept an offer and stay.

And unlike most questions, this one keeps coming back. A recruiter asks it in the first call. A hiring manager asks it again. At both companies, culture or values questions show up in the onsite too, often disguised: "tell me about a time you gave hard feedback" is a culture question at Netflix.

"Why us?" tests homework, fit and intent. Fit is the heavy one: show that you already work the way they say they want people to work.

3How to read a culture document

A culture document is a list of claims ("we share context, not rules"). Each claim implies behaviors ("people decide things themselves, with information"). Each behavior is something you may already have evidence for. So read with three columns:

Claim in the documentBehavior it impliesMy evidence
"Context, not control"Explain the why and let people decide; don't add approval stepsThe release dashboard that replaced the 40-step checklist
Candid feedback is expectedTell a peer directly when something isn't working, kindly and with specificsThe design review where I told our lead the plan wouldn't hit the deadline
………

Two rules while you fill it in. First, only claim the rows where you have real evidence. Three strong rows beat ten thin ones. Second, notice the rows that make you uncomfortable. Both companies say plainly that their culture isn't for everyone, and an interviewer will respect "this part is hard for me, and here's how I handle it" more than pretend agreement.

You're reading a culture document. What's the most useful thing to write down?

Right. The middle column is the bridge: it turns a slogan into something you can have done. Your evidence column becomes your answers.

Picture the interviewer's side. They wrote the document; quoting it back tells them nothing new, and they don't need you to argue that their values are good. What they can't know without asking is whether you act that way. So the useful notes connect each claim to a behavior ("context, not control" means "explains why instead of adding approvals") and then to a real moment you can tell as a story.

4Netflix: the culture memo

Netflix has published a culture document for over fifteen years. It started as a slide deck in 2009, was rewritten as a memo, and was updated again in 2024; read the current version at jobs.netflix.com/culture. Its central bet: hire unusually strong people, give them unusual freedom, and expect unusual results. The ideas that come up most in interviews:

IdeaWhat it means in practiceQuestions it turns into
A dream team, and the keeper testEvery role should be held by someone excellent. Managers ask: if this person told me they were leaving, would I fight to keep them? If not, the memo says to part ways generously. It also asks people to talk to their manager regularly about how they're doing."How do you know you're doing well?" "How do you raise the bar around you?"
Freedom and responsibility, judgmentFew rules and approvals. People are trusted to act in Netflix's best interest and to own the outcome."Tell me about a big decision you made without being told to."
Context, not controlLeaders share the information and the goal, then let people decide, rather than approving every step."How do you get a team to make good decisions without you?"
Highly aligned, loosely coupledAgree hard on goals and strategy; then teams move independently, without cross-team sign-offs for every tactic."How did you work with teams you didn't control?"
CandorFeedback is given often and directly, to peers and managers alike, with the aim of helping. Saying it only behind someone's back is seen as disloyal."Tell me about the hardest feedback you gave, and the hardest you received."
Informed captains, and seeking dissentEach decision has one owner who makes the call, after actively asking for disagreement. Others give their view, then support the decision."When did you make a call others disagreed with?" "When did you commit to one you disagreed with?"

The memo also lists the values it hires for, such as judgment, selflessness, courage and curiosity, with a few sentences describing each. Read them; each one is a story prompt.

What this means for you as a senior engineer: Netflix interviews lean on judgment and candor. Expect behavioral questions about feedback, about decisions you owned end to end, and about disagreeing with someone more senior. A good "why Netflix" is usually about the working model (freedom with context, a high bar, candor), not about the shows.

Go deeper: the honest trade-offs

The memo is open that this culture is demanding: the keeper test means performance that's merely adequate is a reason to part ways, with a generous severance. People who want close direction, or who find direct feedback hard to give or take, often don't enjoy it. You don't need to bring this up, but if an interviewer asks how you feel about it, an honest answer that shows you've thought about it ("I'd rather know where I stand; on my last team I asked for monthly feedback for that reason") is far better than enthusiasm that sounds rehearsed. The book No Rules Rules (Reed Hastings and Erin Meyer, 2020) explains the reasoning at length, if you want more than the memo.

A Netflix interviewer asks, "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision." Which idea from the memo is it mainly probing?

Right. A strong answer has both halves: you raised it directly with data, the owner decided, and you then backed the decision fully (or you were the owner and sought out the dissent).

Ask what behavior the question would reveal. Disagreement is about two moments: speaking up, and what you do after the decision. Speaking up directly is candor; making one person the decider who listens first, then everyone supporting the call, is the informed-captain model. The keeper test is about how managers judge performance, and "aligned, loosely coupled" is about how teams coordinate; neither is what a disagreement story shows best.

5Anthropic: mission, values and the safety documents

Anthropic calls itself an AI safety and research company, and it's a public benefit corporation: a company that is legally allowed, and expected, to weigh a public purpose alongside profit. Its purpose is the responsible development of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity. Where Netflix's culture is about how people work, Anthropic's starts with why the company exists, so "why Anthropic" is mostly a question about the mission. Four sources, all on anthropic.com:

The company page and its values

The company page lists a short set of values. The first is act for the global good: make decisions that are best for humanity in the long run. Another, hold light and shade, means holding both sides at once: AI could go very badly, and it could do enormous good. Read the whole list on the live page; like Netflix's, each value is a story prompt.

Core Views on AI Safety (2023)

This essay explains why the company exists. Its argument, in plain words:

  1. AI is improving fast and could be transformative within a decade or so.
  2. Nobody yet knows how to train very capable systems to be reliably helpful, honest and harmless.
  3. So safety research is urgent, and much of it can only be done by working with frontier models; studying weaker ones isn't enough.
  4. Because it's unclear how hard safety will turn out to be, the company takes a portfolio approach: research that helps whether things turn out easy, medium or very hard.

It names research areas such as interpretability (understanding what happens inside a model), scalable oversight (supervising systems that may be smarter than the supervisor), and testing for dangerous failure modes.

The Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)

The RSP is a public commitment that ties safety measures to how capable a model is. Think of lab safety levels for biology: the more dangerous the material, the stricter the containment, and you may not handle the material until the containment is in place. The RSP defines AI Safety Levels (ASL-2, ASL-3 and up), each with security and deployment safeguards, and capability thresholds that trigger the stricter level. Anthropic activated ASL-3 protections for its most capable models in May 2025, and the policy was rewritten as version 3 in early 2026. Read the current version at anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy.

What this means for you: Anthropic wants people who take the mission seriously and can reason about it, including its tensions. You'll be asked why you want to work on AI safety at an AI company, and the interviewer will be listening for whether you've thought about the trade-off (building capable models to study safety, while worrying about capable models). Honesty matters more than enthusiasm: "I'm not sure the race dynamics work out, and here's why I still think being inside is the better bet" is a stronger answer than unexamined agreement.

In one sentence, what does the Responsible Scaling Policy do?

Right. Capability goes up, safeguards must go up first: the lab-safety-level picture.

Go back to the biology lab picture. A lab safety rule doesn't ban dangerous research; it says that before you handle a more dangerous material, you need stronger containment. The RSP works the same way for AI: AI Safety Levels set what security and deployment protections a model of a given capability needs. It isn't a size cap, and it isn't the values list on the company page, which is a separate document.

Why does Core Views argue that safety research needs frontier models?

Right. That's the core of the bet, and the tension an interviewer may ask you about: to study the risks of capable systems, you have to build capable systems.

Think of crash-testing cars. You can learn a lot from a bicycle, but you can't learn how a car's crumple zone behaves at 100 km/h without crashing a car at 100 km/h. Core Views makes the same argument: behaviors that matter for safety may only appear at the frontier, so research on weaker models alone could miss them. Funding is a real consideration, but it isn't the essay's argument, and the essay doesn't claim small models are safe.

6Build the 90-second answer

Use four beats:

  1. The specific draw: one or two ideas from their documents that matter to you. Name them.
  2. Your evidence: a short story showing you already work that way (30 to 40 seconds; the full story can come later).
  3. What you'd do there: the kind of work or team you want, tied to the role.
  4. An honest edge: optionally, one thing you're curious about or still weighing. It shows thinking, not doubt.

Here's a worked example of the shape (the person and story are made up; yours must be real):

"Two things in the culture memo match how I like to work. One is context, not control. On my last team, releases needed sign-off from three leads, and they were slow and still broke. I built a dashboard that showed each release's risk, changed files, test coverage and on-call load, and let engineers decide. Releases doubled and incidents fell by a third. The other is candor: I asked my team to give me monthly written feedback, and it changed how I ran design reviews. I want to work on the playback and streaming platform side, where those decisions are made at a much bigger scale. One thing I'd like to understand is how the keeper test feels from the inside for engineers on long infrastructure projects."

That's about 90 seconds spoken. It fails the swap test (good), every claim has evidence, and it ends on a real question.

Which "why Anthropic" opening is strongest?

Right. A specific idea, real evidence, and where you'd apply it. A follow-up on what you think about the hard parts of the mission is likely next, so have a view ready.

Run the two tests. The swap test: "AI is the future, best models" fits any AI lab, so it's generic. The evidence test: "I agree with everything" claims fit but shows no behavior and no thinking, and total agreement can sound like you haven't read it closely. The strong answer picks one idea, shows you've done a version of it, and points at the work you'd do.

7Questions to ask the recruiter

The first recruiter call is also your best source of information. Ask, every time:

  • The screen's format: "Is the technical screen algorithmic, practical (building something in stages), or something else? How long, which language, shared editor or IDE, can I run code?"
  • The onsite round list: "Which rounds are in the loop: coding, system design, behavioral, a project deep dive, anything else? Is any round with AI tools allowed?"
  • The team: "Is this for a specific team, or is team matching after the offer?"
  • Level and timeline: "What level is this role? What's the usual time from screen to decision?"

Write the answers down in sections/15-interview-formats/notes.md. They decide which parts of the track get extra time: a practical screen means more week 2 building, and a design-heavy loop means starting the onsite stage early.

Why ask a recruiter for the exact screen format rather than preparing for everything?

Right. It's free information that changes your plan. Recruiters generally share it, because they want you to do well.

Picture studying for an exam without knowing if it's multiple choice or an essay. You'd split your time across both and be half-ready for each. Interview screens differ that much: a 45-minute algorithm problem and a 90-minute four-level build reward different practice. Asking costs nothing, and you're not trying to change the format, only to prepare for the right one.

8What to say in the interview

  1. Name one or two specific ideas from their documents, in your own words.
  2. Back each with a short, real story where you already worked that way.
  3. Say what work you want to do there, tied to the role.
  4. If asked about the hard parts (the keeper test; building AI while worrying about AI), give an honest view, not cheerleading.
  5. End the call by asking for the screen format and the onsite round list.

✓What to remember

  1. "Why us?" tests homework, fit and intent. Generic answers pass the swap test; good ones fail it.
  2. Read culture documents in three columns: claim, behavior, your evidence.
  3. Netflix: dream team and keeper test, freedom and responsibility, context not control, highly aligned loosely coupled, candor, informed captains who seek dissent.
  4. Anthropic: a public benefit corporation built around AI safety; values such as acting for the global good and holding light and shade; Core Views (safety needs frontier research and a portfolio approach); the RSP (safeguards scale with capability, through AI Safety Levels).
  5. 90 seconds: specific draw, your evidence, what you'd do, an honest edge.
  6. Always ask for the screen format and the onsite round list. Read the live documents before each interview.

Warm-up · pen and paper

2. Three columns, both companies

Fill section 3's table for each company: at least five claims each, the behavior, and your evidence. Leave the evidence blank where you have none; don't stretch.

3. Write both answers

Core · speak

4. Say them, timed

Say each answer with a 90-second timer, recorded. Listen back for the swap test (could another company's name go in?) and for "we" where you meant "I". Fix and repeat once.

5. Where do you disagree?

For each company, write one thing in its documents you're unsure about or disagree with, and why, in three sentences. You may never say it in an interview, but having thought it through is what makes your agreement sound real.
